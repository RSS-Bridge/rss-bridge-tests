Upgrade Your CoCo The Modern Way

2026-11-12 00:00:00

<div class="entry-content"> <p>The Radio Shack TRS-80 Colour Computer may never have achieved the sales volume of its competitors from Commodore or Apple, but it offered a surprisingly capable set of hardware. There were three versions, with the final one being a significant upgrade in many ways. There was no upgrade path for owners of version 1 and 2 machines to have a version 3 back in the day, but now thanks to [Felipe Antoniosi] such an upgrade is possible. How? <a href="https://github.com/lfantoniosi/CoCoTANG" target="_blank">With a Tang Nano 20K FPGA board</a>, of course!</p> <p>The CoCo 3 had a paged memory system for expansion up to 512k, as well as a significantly more capable graphics chip which while compatible with the original Motorola part offered enhanced graphics. The upgrade emulates these, and connects to the CoCo via a set of daughter boards that connect to where the various motherboard chips sat. it offers an HDMI output as well as analog video and enhanced sound capability, bringing the machine firmly into the 21st century.</p> <p>As mods go to classic hardware, this is quite an invasive one. Whether or not you’d prefer to do this to your CoCo is up to you, but ask yourself this, would you have done this had this board been available in the 1980s? Of course you would.</p> <p>Meanwhile should you become bored with the CoCo, <a href="https://hackaday.com/2024/03/02/its-a-coco-no-its-an-apple-ii/">it can also be persuaded to emulate an Apple ][</a>.</p> </div>

    • Baochip-1x: A Mostly-Open, 22nm SoC For High Assurance Applications

    2026-11-12 00:00:00

    <div class="entry-content"> <p>For most people, just getting any old microcontroller or system on chip (SoC) from our favorite – or cheapest – vendor is more than good enough. But if you’re really into high assurance computing where every transistor has to be accounted for, you got a bit of a problem. This is basically why [Andrew “Bunnie” Huang] <a href="https://www.bunniestudios.com/blog/2026/baochip-1x-a-mostly-open-22nm-soc-for-high-assurance-applications/" target="_blank">pitched in and had the Baochip-1x produced</a>.</p> <p><img decoding="async" data-attachment-id="1425025" data-permalink="https://hackaday.com/2026/11/12/baochip-1x-a-mostly-open-22nm-soc-for-high-assurance-applications/baochip-logo/" data-orig-file="https://hackaday.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/baochip-logo.png" data-orig-size="213,213" data-comments-opened="1" data-image-title="baochip-logo" data-image-description="" data-image-caption="" data-large-file="https://hackaday.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/baochip-logo.png?w=213" class="size-medium wp-image-1425025 alignright" src="https://hackaday.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/baochip-logo.png?w=213" alt="" width="213" height="213"/></p> <p>This SoC’s design is based heavily on the FPGA-based CPU in the <a href="https://hackaday.com/2022/08/06/heres-how-the-precursor-protects-your-privacy/">Precursor device</a>, which itself is claimed to be a fully verifiable communicator design. Since it does have an MMU, you can run any software on it that supports the RV32IMAC RISC-V instruction set, with a <a href="https://baochip.github.io/baochip-1x/" target="_blank">Coder’s Guide</a> available for those who might feel interested in this SoC, along with the SystemVerilog and Verilog-based <a href="https://github.com/baochip/baochip-1x" target="_blank">sources</a>.</p> <p>In addition to the DIY assembly process, you can even get a physical hardware version, including the ‘Dabao’ evaluation board with a very Raspberry Pi Pico-shaped form factor. Inside this SoC is a single-core, 350 MHz VexRISC-V core, 4 MB resistive RAM (RRAM) and 2 MB of SRAM, along with a whole heap of security-related features.</p> </div>

We didn’t find the actual code for the patches, but we still thought the detective work was worth the read.     • Tiny Transmitters Track Monarch Butterflies

    2026-11-12 00:00:00

    <div class="entry-content"> <p>Miniaturization is one thing, but how would you like to design a transmitter that attaches to a butterfly? That’s what the <a href="https://www.begiant.ca/stories/places/monarch-butterflies-tracking-conservation" target="_blank">BlūMorpho</a> transmitter is made to do, according to [Tom Jokinen] writing for <em>Be Giant</em>. Apparently, most of these butterflies have a two- to six-week life, but periodically a “super generation” spawns and lives up to nine months. This is enough time to migrate from Canada and the United States to Mexico. In Mexico, they spend the winter, and then return when the weather is more hospitable. Scientists wanted to study this migration pattern, and the tiny transmitter is exactly what they needed.</p> <p>You can even <a href="https://projectmonarch.org/#app" target="_blank">download an app</a> to track the butterflies yourself. The transmitter looks like a tadpole and weighs 60 milligrams. There’s no battery, but there is a tiny solar cell. The transmitter emits a Bluetooth signal that your phone’s location services can relay.</p> <p>To affix the transmitter, researchers put a glassine envelope over the butterfly, weigh its wings down with something slightly heavy, and then apply eyelash glue to the transmitter. You can see the process in the video below.</p> <p>What else could you do with a virtually weightless Bluetooth tracker? It is possible that dedicated tracking stations could <a href="https://hackaday.com/2026/10/24/pushing-bluetooth-out-to-12-kilometers/">have a longer range</a>.</p> <p> <a href="https://hackaday.com/2026/11/12/tiny-transmitters-track-monarch-butterflies/#more-1424900" class="more-link">Continue reading <span class="screen-reader-text">“Tiny Transmitters Track Monarch Butterflies”</span> <span class="meta-nav">→</span></a></p> </div>
