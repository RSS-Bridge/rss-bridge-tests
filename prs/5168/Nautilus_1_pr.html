Our Planet’s Lungs Are Suffering

2026-10-29 20:00:00

by: Jake Currie

The Amazon Rainforest is often referred to as the “lungs of the planet” because its lush canopy “inhales” carbon dioxide and “exhales” oxygen. This breathing pattern is part of an important natural defense against climate change, pulling carbon from the atmosphere and sequestering it, but that might be changing. According to a new study published in Nature, the Amazon may shift from a net sink to a net source of atmospheric carbon.

A team of climate scientists arrived at that conclusion after analyzing atmospheric samples taken by planes criss-crossing the skies over the Amazon for 15 years. Satellite-based monitoring is difficult because of the dense blanket of cloud cover generated by evaporating moisture and ground-based systems are few and far between. So the team went straight to the source. 

Read more: “The Rainforest Is Teeming with Consciousness”  

“In this context, airborne vertical profiling fills a critical gap, providing the most direct way to quantify the movement of carbon between tropical land ecosystems and the atmosphere at the regional scale,” study author Jen Johnson of the University of Kansas explained in a statement.

The team divided the15 years studied into three different groups: wet periods with little human interference, periods of heavy deforestation but minimal climate stressors, and periods marked by acute climate stress (droughts, wildfires, and El Niños).   

“We wanted to know, ‘Is the effect of global climate on the Amazon the most important factor determining its carbon balance, or are regional human-driven activities—like deforestation and clearing through wildfires—equally or more important?’” Johnson said.

The team found that during wet years, when the forests were left relatively unperturbed, the Amazon was a net source of carbon, but just a small one. Things got much worse in other years. Carbon emissions spiked during years when the climate was mild but deforestation increased. Years where high levels of human activity and climate stressors coincided were the worst of all. 

In other words, whether it’s from wildfires or deforestation, human activity is transforming the Amazon from a net carbon sink into a net carbon source with potentially disastrous consequences for climate change. 

“For many decades, there has been concern about the idea of a tipping point, where gradual drying and gradual loss of the forest could feed back on itself in a self-reinforcing way,” Johnson said. “That could create a transition from an ecosystem type that has stored huge amounts of carbon in living vegetation to drier forests or other ecosystem types that do not store nearly as much carbon.”

The solution, per Johnson, is relatively simple: stop destroying the trees. “Measures to bring deforestation down to zero are required to manage net carbon emissions from the Amazon,” she said.

The alternative is finding out how to live on a planet suffering from chronic lung disease.

    • Dear Human, Poetry Might Save Us from the Apocalypse

    2026-10-29 19:06:37

    by: Kristen French

    When we tell the story
    of how we survived the collapse, 
    we might say: 
    like birds, we learned
    to move as one.
    We grew lighter
    and lengthened our wings.

    These are the final lines of “Dear Human at the Edge of Time: A Community Poem.” Assembled from the contributions of 14 different writers across the United States, the poem tells a story about the future that is simultaneously devastating and hopeful.

    In the three years since the poem was penned, the question of how we might collectively survive collapse has become an increasingly pressing one. It turns out, we are not paying enough attention to the birds. We have not learned to move as one.

    The poem was published in 2023 by Poets for Science in connection with the Fifth National Climate Assessment, which revealed that climate change was already ravaging every region of the United States through rising temperatures, punishing drought, savage wildfires, and deadly storms.

    Read more: “Poetry Gives Us Power”

    But humanity continues to fall short of the change needed to keep the Earth on a safe course for future generations. The data on climate change have told a clear story for a long time, but that story is not getting through.

    Could art and poetry dedicated to conservation themes help ignite a greater feeling of urgency about our predicament and map a way forward? Poets for Science, forged in the turmoil of the first Trump administration, has made this call to action one of its missions.

    The group is uniting poets and scientists in collaborations that aim to make the science of conservation both felt viscerally and understood intellectually. It’s also inviting the public to participate with verse or other creative work of their own.

    The poem “Dear Human” lives on an interactive platform that allows anyone to contribute. Writers can respond to poems published in an award-winning climate poetry anthology, add to a digital gallery of climate art, or build their own “found poems” out of text borrowed from the Fifth National Climate Assessment.

    “Why not poetry?” says poet David Hassler, who co-founded Poets for Science together with American nature poet Jane Hirshfield in 2017. In just a couple of weeks, Poets for Science will hold its first in-person gathering, where scientists and poets will meet across three days to explore the connections between their disciplines, and ways to inspire the civic imagination. That meeting is sponsored by the Wick Poetry Center at Kent State University in Ohio, which Hassler directs.

    I spoke with Hassler recently about how poetry is like lichen—and how it can help make science meaningful while inspiring collective action.

    There have been many periods across history when art and science were closely aligned. Artists were scientists and scientists were artists. Nobel laureate neuroscientist Eric Kandel beautifully describes such a period in Vienna at the turn of the 20th century in his book     The Age of Insight. The Renaissance, too, was packed with figures who merged artistic mastery with scientific inquiry. But during the scientific revolution, art and science split. There was this intellectual divide. Today it seems like the pendulum might be swinging back the other way. What do you make of this shift?

    Yes, all these collective efforts have been co-arising all around the globe, people pursuing similar intersections of science and humanities and arts. It’s what we have discovered in pulling together this Poets for Science gathering. You know, we’re not alone. When we started Poetry for Science in 2017, we were tapping into a collective nerve that I think is shared by so many people. And it’s really been growing over the past 10 to 15 years.

    In a recent interview, I was reminded that the word “scientist” was coined in the mid 1800s. Before that, they were “natural philosophers.” There’s this divide between our belief that over here’s my rational thinking, and over there’s my emotional thinking. Here’s my scientific inquiry and over there’s my creative expression.

    This divide has been exacerbated by our biases and our technology. I think that it has come to a breaking point, where people are hungering to integrate facts with feelings, so that what we’re learning through the facts of science has meaning in our lives, emotionally and in our lived experience. Perhaps this need to be intentional about bringing them back together in conversation has happened out of an awareness of our failure at progress. We’re feeling sicker. We’re feeling more divided, more alone, more unhealthy, and there’s been a kind of quiet desperation.

    We’re also looking for creative solutions to intractable problems in our collective lives and AI is making us question what it means to be human. There’s this attack on the humanities, and tech bros are saying we just need to be good bots. Maybe there’s a moment here where we’re infusing the arts back into our rational scientific thinking because we need to remind ourselves what it means to be human and what we value about being human. Scientists are trying to remember that the questions they ask and the work they do is informed by their humanness, by their cultural background, their emotional lives, their beliefs, their lived experience.

    Do you think that poetry does something for science or for public understanding of science that other art forms can’t?

    I think all art forms can help us make meaning and carry grief and hold our sense of awe and to use that as an energy source, to contain it and to give it shape. All art can do that.

    But here’s what poetry can do: It can take the natural metaphors of science and highlight them. We’re not inventing them. If I say that we’re having a “mycelial” conversation, you know what I mean, because we now understand how a mycorrhizal network in soil helps trees communicate with each other and share signals of nurture, but also warning, through this fungal network, this mycorrhizal network.

    I think that the language we use to describe the world not only influences how we behave toward the world, it truly influences what we’re able to actually see in the world. The root word of science means to know from Latin, and I think the economy of language in a poem helps us carry new knowledge into the world. And that’s gone a long way. We have been told over and over again that poets are now feeling inspired and getting prompts for their writing from scientific articles and using the language of science in their poems.

    But it’s working both ways. Scientists are saying they’re turning to poetry. We have so many poems on the poetsforscience.org global gallery and in this anthology The Nature of Our Times written by scientists. Why? They say that they don’t have a place in their scientific paper or essay to put their emotional language. The poems offer a place to put their awe and grief and fear. Many scientists also say they’re using the arts and humanities to help them shape the questions they ask and the directions they go in their scientific inquiry. Laura Valski, for instance, talks about how in her research with ecology [in Washington State], she’s attuned to the stories of the native people of that region and of the language they use to inform the questions she asks. 

    I love what Jane Hirshfield says, that the metaphor and the microscope are both instruments of discovery.

    Yes, metaphor stems from metapheran in Greek, its origin. It means to carry, to cross over. In a sense, we’re carrying new knowledge over from science into our emotional lives through language and metaphor. And that’s how it can bring change.

    Obviously, science is many things. Not just conservation, but also technology, so it’s AI and surveillance cameras, or it’s building weapons.

    Yes, and some kinds of poetry are used by dictators to influence mass crowds and to hypnotize people. The spell of poetry can be used for good and for bad. So can science. Both of them are that powerful.

    How do you grapple with this darker side of science and poetry when you encourage collaborations?

    The 14th century poet Rumi said your intention does all the work. We do it with care and with the right intention and with rigor and with honesty. I’m not writing a poem to influence your behavior for my gain. I’m writing a poem because I have an intention to speak a truth that is authentic to me. The paradox is that if I can speak authentically and truthfully about my own idiosyncratic personal life, only then can it have a power that speaks to others and help them find their own meaning from it.

    So, yes, we can create nuclear weapons and toxic chemicals. I just watched this frightening documentary about the pesticides that we’re using in agriculture that are destroying our soil, our health, and our bodies. Those chemicals were invented by a German scientist for warfare in World War II. The companies literally took the research that was being done for warfare and tweaked it to use on our crops. How’s that for an image?

    In the essay you write that art without science risks inspiring ineffective action. Why is that?

    If I don’t understand how mycelial networks actually work and I wanna write a poem about mycelium, then perhaps what I’m writing might work as a poem, but it doesn’t help me understand the forest and act in ways that create the conditions for that forest to thrive. We have a crisis of science communication, which was the origin of the Poets for Science movement in 2017, so poems can help us make meaning and speak to our own lived experience and our emotional lives. And by doing that, to make meaning of science.

    This Poets for Science initiative began in response to the actions of the first Trump administration, and out of desperation, anger, grief, and fear. Jane Hirshfield wrote a poem, and that poem went around the world essentially in 2017. And then she called me, and said, “Can you help me?” And I said, “Of course.” And our design team put together 23 seven-foot banners of poems about science that she curated. We went to the National Mall for the very first March for Science on Earth Day. Poets for Science had a teach-in tent. There was never room for anybody to sit because it was constantly full the whole day.

    What do successful poems about conservation like Hirshfield’s get right?

    I avoid rating this poem over that poem and saying this poem succeeds and that poem fails. I go back to the intention of the poet and the context in which the poem is presented. I work a lot in the schools with fourth graders and eighth graders and I’ve seen the work of poems happen. We write poems to change ourselves. The poem becomes a kind of residue, it marks a new insight.

    The poems that seem to translate the experience of the poet to another listener use metaphors grounded in authentic truth. The language has specificity to it. The metaphor works at multiple levels. The figurative language accurately describes or points to, for instance, how root systems in the soil communicate and support one another toward the collective health of a forest. Ideally, this language has the vibrancy of a true conversation, which from Latin “conveyor” means “together to turn.” We forget when reading where your idea ends and my idea begins. We’re caught up in this decentralized collective effort to move an idea further or to hit upon phrasing for an expression.

    Are there poems either from The Nature of Our Times or otherwise, that you feel meaningfully changed a conservation outcome? Or would you not measure it in that way?

    There’s a great phrase that Jane Hirshfield used: She says perhaps poems are the lichens of activism. And one of the things about lichens is that the work they’re doing is often very, very, very slow, often subtle, and often unseen. I think the same can be true of poems. It’s not so easy to say, “Ah, two weeks later, you changed your behavior.” And there’s something about the arts and poetry that resists scientific inquiry.

    So this is also pushing against a tension that I think is interesting. So many questions of the spirit are not answerable or provable. Donald Hall says, “Poems are the unsayable said.” What he’s pointing at is the tyranny of what we can measure. Rebecca Solnit talks about this. We can be tyrannized by only believing in what we can absolutely measure. And then suddenly, we’re feeling deprived of spiritual life, because that’s what we can’t measure.

    I think poems flirt with that notion of “let’s be patient like lichen. Let’s be tolerant of things that don’t have an easy answer.” Maybe the work of lichens is that work of breaking rocks, slowly making soil. It’s the creative, productive work of decomposition. There’s an element of poems breaking our rigid ways of thinking and feeling and opening up new possibilities that we don’t quite fit into yet. We don’t know when that’s gonna change our behavior. We never quite are certain about it. But they’re pulling us in and they’re making us pause. And in that pause, we suddenly have room for new thought or for making a connection we would never have made before or for simply questioning a very rigid way of thinking that we always carried for so long.

    Towards the end of the essay, you suggest that we need research into which kinds of artistic approaches and collaborations could have real impact. 

    What I’m saying is take caution. Research with patience. This is not supposed to be easy. We don’t need to be overly ambitious. James Pennebaker’s research from 30 years ago about journaling and the power of using language to process grief, it moved the needle. It wasn’t overshooting in its statements. How do we find the appropriate way to engage in research that can bring other people into the conversation? We’re a small group, the world of poets. Talk about a bubble. Nobody inside it needs convincing. “Look at this poem. Oh, I love that poem. You know, that reminds me of this poem.” Nobody’s asking, “Where’s your data?”

    If you were to fantasize about a first study in this vein, one that can sort out what kinds of collaborations between poets and scientists might change behavior, what would it look like?

    I’ve spent 30 years going into the community. At the Wick Poetry Center, we like to say that we bring poetry to people’s everyday lives. We want people to encounter a poem who would never go to a bookstore and buy a small book of poems. They’d never think of themselves as writers or readers of poetry. We love to introduce it to them in a friendly way that invites them in.

    During the pandemic, we worked with nurses and we brought poems about nursing for an incredible project we called “Sacred Breath,” and another project called “Some Days.” And we invited nurses to write their own reflections inspired by a model poem about their experiences. And we didn’t say it has to be a poem, but many of them ended up writing beautiful phrases and images in metaphors that is the work of poetry. They weren’t fearful that they could be judged. We pulled them into a conversation through poetry about their profession and their life experiences that were emotionally charged and needed an outlet. Why not do that with the environmental movement or with climate change? Could we set up a group where we’re studying how it affects people’s ways of thinking? When we change our thinking, we change our behavior.

    What impact are you hoping the first Poetry for Science gathering in a few weeks will have?

    We have this incredible group of featured speakers: Phillip Levin, Jane Lubchenco, the former head of NOAA, Melissa Nelson, who’s an Indigenous environment ecologist. And wonderful poets. We will be celebrating this collective work that so many of us have been doing. We were inspired to create this intentional space and time for people to come together because we were learning about this work happening in so many areas around our country and around the world. Our hope is that we create the time and the space for people to meet one another, to share ideas, to be inspired by each other, and then leave the gathering and have new relationships that can help us form new collaborations.

    We’re creating an online community directory where people can, if they wish, post their project, their research interest and their contact information, either as poet, artist or scientist, and it can be searchable. We want to provide more momentum to this collective movement that has been building for 14 or 15 years in a decentralized way. Nobody’s ever called it out and named it until now. There is power in naming, and this Poets for Science gathering is halfway between an academic conference and a festival. It’s a gathering because we’re gathering the resources that we all carry within us and we want people to share their expertise, their knowledge, their passion with each other.

    • Men Who Eat More Plant-Based Protein Have Higher Testosterone Levels

    2026-10-29 17:58:46

    by: Jake Currie

    There’s a testosterone crisis in America—at least according to a number of Trump administration officials. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., for example, has bemoaned the low-T of the American youth, and even boosts his own levels with testosterone replacement therapy. He’s also a major proponent of eating animals, celebrating the prominent position of meat and dairy in the new food pyramid the US Department of Agriculture unveiled at the beginning of this year.

    But a study published last month in the International Journal of Food Sciences and Nutrition suggests Kennedy might be able to ease up on the testosterone shots if he ate more vegetables.

    A team of nutritional and metabolic scientists analyzed the four-day dietary intake and hormone levels of more than 2,500 middle-aged men living in Finland. It’s worth noting that the data in their study were also middle-aged, collected during the Kuopio Ischaemic Heart Disease Risk Factor Study between 1984 and 1989.

    Read more: “The Impossible Strength of the Testosterone Myth”

    After dividing the men into four groups according to their protein intake and controlling for other variables, the researchers found that the subjects’ testosterone levels seemed to be linked to the kind of protein they preferred. On average, the men who ate more meat and dairy had slightly lower testosterone levels. In contrast, those who got more of their protein from plant-based sources had slightly higher levels of the sex hormone. The scientists observed a similar dynamic with their levels of sex hormone binding globulin, a protein that controls how much free testosterone is available in the blood stream.

    Drilling down into the dietary data, the team discovered dairy was most responsible for the relatively low-T in the animal protein group. The plant protein group’s higher testosterone levels showed a closer association with protein from vegetables, rather than cereals.

    The researchers said they aren’t entirely sure why the protein source appears to affect male sex hormone levels, but said the pattern may be mediated by inflammation. Higher levels of C-reactive protein, a molecule involved in inflammatory pathways, have been linked to reduced overall testosterone levels and those who eat more plant protein typically have lower levels of C-reactive protein.

    Additionally, while the researchers controlled for a number of health and lifestyle variables, it’s possible that the plant-eating group had healthier habits overall. But because this is a cross-sectional observational study, causation is impossible to pin down.

    Of course, there are plenty of other reasons to limit meat intake—both for your own health and the planet’s. In fact, a new international food pyramid released this week recommends getting the bulk of your calories from plants instead of animals. Testosterone-maxxing might just be an attractive bonus.

    • Your Cells Keep a Record of Every Workout You’ve Ever Done

    2026-10-29 13:59:21

    by: Diego Hernandez-Saavedra

    Have you ever taken months off training—maybe due to an injury, a new baby, or crushing work deadlines—and come back convinced you were starting from zero?

    Research from our team has some good news: At the cellular level, you probably weren’t.

    We found that muscle and liver cells store memories from every training session in ways that may help improve endurance, control blood sugar levels, and even slow weight regain after inactivity.

    Use it or lose it? Maybe not

    For most of the past century, long before fad diets and fancy workout trends took off, exercise was considered one of the most effective lifestyle changes to lower the risk of heart disease, Type 2 diabetes, several cancers and early death for people of all ages. But people typically understood exercise as something whose beneficial effects fade away over time: You use it or lose it.

    When you stop training, your maximum oxygen uptake, muscle gains and insulin sensitivity drift back to where they started, often within weeks. Physical activity guidelines are built on this fact, which is why they emphasize consistency above all else.

    Consistency, however, is a luxury that roughly 3 in 4 American adults aren’t able to enjoy. People can experience injuries, illness, pregnancy or jobs that limit their time for or ability to exercise. If adaptation really resets, every time you step into the gym would be as if you’ve never been there before. But that scenario doesn’t match what coaches and returning athletes typically describe: In practice, people who have trained before can get fit again faster than those who haven’t undergone similar training.

    In recent years, however, scientists have started challenging the concept of “use it or lose it.” New research hints that your cells might keep a record of your training history so all of your gains are not completely lost.

    More than muscle

    We are exercise physiologists who study how cells remember exercise. Our lab and others have shown that the body is able to store a form of exercise memory across different organs.

    To study how this exercise memory works, our team of researchers use an ON-OFF-ON exercise training protocol in mice, which involves an initial four- to eight-week training period, followed by detraining—a period of no exercise—and a second training bout. This method allows us to examine how cells form a memory of exercise, maintain a record of exercise through detraining, and recall memories of prior training when exposed again to exercise.

    Using this strategy, our team found that skeletal muscle memory accelerates muscle growth. Compared to mice with no training history, mice who retrained grew bigger muscles, and the individual muscle fibers inside those muscles grew too. Larger muscle fibers meant that the working cells were themselves getting bigger, rather than the surrounding tissue.

    Looking more closely at these muscle cells, we found that retrained muscle is better at matching energy production to demand—meaning, the muscles were better at drawing from energy stores when it’s needed most—and this ability persists without further exercise. This memory held even when the mice ate a high-fat diet, suggesting that prior training can still offer benefits even when diet is working against those gains.

    Muscle isn’t the only body part that learns from exercise. Working muscle needs fuel, and the liver supplies it during and after training sessions.

    My colleagues and I found that liver cells also store exercise memory and uses it to feed muscle more efficiently. In both mice and people who trained, stopped, then trained again, their livers released higher levels of a family of enzymes called carboxylesterases. These enzymes chop up lipids circulating in the blood, delivering fat in a form that muscle can take up and burn as it grows.

    A record that outlives you

    Recorded cellular memories of exercise may even be passed down to future generations.

    In mice, we found that three to four weeks of maternal and paternal endurance exercise can have a lasting effect on their offspring. Compared to offspring born to sedentary parents, parental exercise granted protection against poor blood sugar levels and excess weight gain for an entire year after birth.

    Researchers have hypothesized numerous ways that prenatal exercise may affect offspring. For example, maternal exercise might allow offspring to spend more time developing in the womb or lead parents to producer higher quality milk.

    But there remains much to be discovered on how parents influence the health of their children and whether their lifestyle could provide a fitness advantage across generations.

    Every cell remembers

    Biology has traditionally granted memory to a short list of cells. Neurons store memory in the connections between them. Immune cells store memory so they can recognize pathogens faster and more strongly the next time they encounter them.

    Researchers previously considered all other cells to be memoryless; a cell responds to a signal, then returns to baseline without keeping a record of that interaction. But that assumption is now coming apart.

    Skin stem cells can keep a record of inflammation, allowing them to heal a new wound faster months later. Human fat cells keep a record of obesity that persists even after substantial weight loss, which may account for why you gain weight faster when switching back to a high-calorie diet—the so-called yo-yo effect.

    We propose that every cell in the body remembers exercise, regardless of how little or how much someone trains. The question is how long these records are stored and whether researchers could prolong or expand each cell’s storage capacity. Better understanding of how cells learn and remember exercise, where they store memories and how those memories are preserved could help improve exercise guidelines for those who cannot exercise for prolonged or consistent periods of time.

    To be clear, none of these findings are evidence for skipping the gym; nothing in our data suggests the effects of past training can substitute for present training. But there is hope that the work you put into getting in shape isn’t completely lost over time.

    Somewhere, deep in your cells, there is a record waiting to be replayed the next time you lace up your running shoes, pick up a pair of dumbbells or want to fulfill a New Year’s resolution.

    • Behold a Prehistoric Lizard Tail Trapped in Amber

    2026-10-29 12:00:00

    by: David Nield

    If you’ve got an escape trick that works, stick with it: A lizard tail trapped in amber reveals that these reptiles were using the same evasion tactic—lose the tail, regrow it later—almost 100 million years ago.

    Many species of modern lizard will detach their own tails if they get grabbed by a bird, snake, or other predator. The tail can keep wriggling as a distraction once it's been shed, allowing the lizard to make a clean getaway—with a replacement tail eventually growing back.

    Now, researchers have found a discarded tail in a block of amber that dates back 98.8 million years, to the time of the Late Cretaceous when dinosaurs roamed the Earth. The piece of amber analyzed by researchers measures just 18 millimeters (0.7 inches) across, so we’re talking fingertip size. The artifact has been in the Steinhardt Museum of Natural History in Israel since 2022, but has only just been described in a newly published study.

    “I realized that what seemed to be a twig was covered in tiny scales,” study author Dolev Fabrikant said in a statement.

    The next step was to grab high-resolution, microscopic images of the tail and compare its features to modern lizards. This revealed the muscle tissue structure characteristic of tail autotomy, with no signs of the damage that would come with an injury.

    These detachments work via fracture planes in the vertebrae, weak points that can be pushed loose through muscle contractions. However, with only the tail to analyze, it’s not clear what precise prehistoric species lost this particular appendage.

    “Although we can securely identify this specimen as a lizard, we lack the skull and other skeletal features that would allow a more secure identification,” Apostolos Christopoulos, one of the study authors, explained in a statement. “Possible affinities include the groups containing today’s wall lizards or, alternatively, slow worms and monitor lizards.”

    We also don’t know if the lizard actually made its escape all those millions of years ago—only that it shed a part of itself in a bid for freedom.

    • Dead Star Offers a Glimpse Into Our Solar System’s Future

    2026-10-28 22:00:00

    by: Jake Currie

    A team of astronomers using the European Southern Observatory’s Very Large Telescope recently spotted something curious. Observing a white dwarf (J1328−1450) located roughly 1,600 light-years from Earth, the team noticed it was surrounded by a rare disc of debris. According to their analysis, recently published in Nature Astronomy, it could offer a glimpse into the future of our solar system. 

    When a star like our sun approaches the end of its life, it first expands into a red giant, then sheds its outer layers, collapsing into a superdense white dwarf. During the dramatic expansion, any closely orbiting planets may be engulfed and torn apart (R.I.P. Earth). The team says this disc of debris surrounding white dwarf J1328−1450 is likely made up of the remnants of those planets.

    Read more: “This Lion-Like Nebula Has Cosmic Dust for a Mane and a Dying Star for a Face”

    But this disc has an interesting twist—or rather, two of them. Instead of a single homogenous ring of swirling cosmic rubble, it’s two distinct rings separated by a gap. The differing chemical compositions of the rings—one rich in magnesium and the other devoid of it—indicate they came from different celestial bodies. The rest of the elements they detected (calcium, oxygen, and iron) suggest the rocky planets that were obliterated were similar to those in our solar system.

    “The system is a rare glimpse of the violent, chaotic process by which a planetary system meets its end, and it hints that dead stars can have disc structures far more complex than previously realized,” study author Gentile Fusillo of Italy’s Università di Trieste explained in a statement.

    But why are there two rings instead of a single dusty disc? 

    The complex double structure may hint at the fates of the planets. It’s likely that these two rings are maintained by surviving planetary bodies, one the size of a typical asteroid for the inner ring and a Pluto-sized planetoid for the outer ring. Just a couple of lonely stellar gravekeepers swirling in a celestial cemetery.

    It bears an eerie resemblance to how our solar system was born. Eons ago, the sun’s gravitational pull attracted a cloud of swirling dust that flattened into a protoplanetary disc. Eventually this dust cloud coalesced into spheroid planets, which continued their orbit around the host star. It seems eons from now the process may play in reverse.

    You know what they say: ashes to ashes, cosmic dust to dust.

    • Vultures Help Eliminate Pathogens. Bird Flu Could Eliminate Them

    2026-10-28 21:00:00

    by: Jake Currie

    Vultures might not be the most charismatic birds, but they do perform an important job. As carrion-feeding scavengers, they dispose of decaying animal matter, even bones, right down their throats. As part of this garbage collection duty, they also neutralize pathogens, routinely gobbling up tissue that’s festering with rabies, anthrax, and botulism and disposing of it in their highly acidic stomachs. 

    Of course, vultures aren’t entirely invulnerable to infection. With 16 of 23 vulture species already designated as threatened, the H5N1 avian flu could wreak havoc on their dwindling numbers. Now a team of biologists is sounding the alarm about what declining vulture populations could mean for ecosystems (and us) and issuing a call to action in a letter published in Conservation Biology.

    Read more: “We’ll Miss the Vulture”

    As carrion feeders, vultures are on the frontlines of the H5N1 panzootic (animal pandemic). They’re not just at risk of contracting the virus from their meals, but from the other vultures who gather around carcasses. Sharp upticks in infections could cause precarious populations to collapse. 

    “The greater the number of infected carcasses, the greater the risk of contagion for the vultures,” letter author Jon Morant of the University of the Basque Country explained in a statement. “Since these carcasses can become sources of infection, a paradox arises: As more vultures die, the capacity to eliminate the carrion reduces, the pathogens spread and contagion increases.”

    But there’s hope for stalling this positive feedback loop, and the authors outlined a series of steps conservationists and wildlife managers can take to fight back. First, vulture populations should be closely monitored for signs of infection, with special attention paid to migratory species that cross international borders, like the Egyptian vulture. Populations that are most threatened should be vaccinated, the team says, noting that there’s already a vaccination program in place for the California condor. 

    Next, vulture feeding stations should be well-stocked with uninfected meat and those that work with vulture populations should suit up. “They must comply strictly with biosecurity protocols (use gloves, masks and protective equipment), especially when working with small populations or with threatened species,” Morant said. The team also recommends that these steps and any additional actions are implemented in a context-sensitive manner that takes into account the socioeconomic conditions of the countries where vultures live.

    H5N1 isn’t just a threat to birds, it can currently infect cattle, pigs, and domestic pets, and every new host the virus adds to the list becomes one more opportunity to jump into humans. By protecting our frontline carcass-eating defenders, we can also help protect ourselves.

    • The Quest for Wisdom in Turbulent Times

    2026-10-28 20:00:00

    by: Kristen French

    For more than a decade a group of Harvard professors has taught a class about wisdom and its religious, moral, and aesthetic dimensions to undergraduates. The four professors are friends from distinct disciplines:­ Michael Puett, a historian of religions; Arthur Kleinman, a physician and medical anthropologist; Stephanie Paulsell, a theologian and literary scholar; and Davíd Carrasco, a historian and anthropologist.

    Inside and outside of the classroom, they have sought to understand how their own areas of study might relate to the art of living well and seeking wisdom during both ordinary and extraordinary times. Now they have written a book that outlines some of the insights they have shared with students and what they have learned while teaching the class, The Practice of Wisdom: Five Quests. These quests wind through the labyrinthine challenges of caregiving, solitude, self-understanding, and transcendence. 

    Read more: “What Is Intelligence?”

    Wisdom, they note in their introduction, is plural. There are many distinctive forms it can take, depending on the place and time one inhabits and the forms of suffering they face. I spoke with Puett, Kleinman, and Paulsell about how they define wisdom, why one should avoid romanticizing wisdom, what solitude and ritual can contribute to the practice, and what kind of wisdom might be required for our current moment.

    I wanted to invite each of you to briefly define wisdom.

    Arthur Kleinman: Aristotle said there were three forms of knowledge or wisdom. One was episteme, how we know things. Then there was techne, or craft, the technical knowledge that is so important to our work. But he thought the most important of all was practical knowledge about how to live a life, which he called phronesis.

    And that last kind is my definition. I think we went out of our way at the beginning of the book in the introduction to explain that we did not feel that any of us is a master of a particular type of wisdom, nor that we were conveying this wisdom as a truth to students and readers. We believe that wisdoms differ, and people’s use of them would differ. We wanted to offer examples based on our own work that emphasized what we think is a universal process: the quest for wisdom.

    Stephanie Paulsell: Yes, we didn’t want to present wisdom as something you could print on a page and say, “This is it,” but to invite our students into practices that would help them figure out ways of living wisely and compassionately in the midst of ordinary life. We presented to them many different writers and artists and musicians in the hopes that they would experiment with these ideas in their own lives.

    Arthur tells this great story in the book about watching this other little boy when he’s young and thinking, “That little boy’s a lot gentler than I am.” And he thinks to himself, “Could I be like that person and still be myself?” That’s a kind of quest for wisdom—not taking on wholesale what we’re offering them, but asking, “Could I engage this in my life and still be myself?”

    Michael Puett: The piece of it that I was trying to bring in, both in the course and in our book, was from the classical Chinese tradition, where one of the opening assumptions is that, at least to our experience, the world we live in is fundamentally capricious and there are no absolute answers. There are no clear norms that will tell us what to do. And that’s not a metaphysical claim. Maybe there are absolute answers, but we don’t know them. It’s therefore all about the practice, the training of a life. And part of what you’re doing is constantly trying to break perspective, learn to see the dangers of assumptions you’re making precisely by constantly having practices that allow you to rethink them, training yourself to learn to operate in complex situations better.

    The book is broken up into five different chapters, and each chapter is about a different kind of quest. What is it about the quest that is so essential to developing wisdom? Are there people who simply possess wisdom by virtue of social position or outsider status, like wise fools or the jesters of Shakespeare’s plays?

    Arthur Kleinman: Anyone living through the current period would be very careful to regard fools as wise. Because we have a leader who’s clearly a fool and is not wise.

    Stephanie Paulsell: The quest metaphor points to the fact that this is an ongoing, lifelong practice. In quest narratives, you go out hoping you’re going to arrive somewhere. But in fact, the quest usually just keeps going and it changes and surprises you, and you end up in places you didn’t expect to be. So the quest metaphor fits very well what we understand wisdom to be. At different moments in our lives, there are different practices that need to come to bear on what we’re doing.

    Michael Puett: In the chapter of the book that I wrote, which is about the quest for the self, I’m building on an amazing Chinese tradition, where the idea is we’re all born messes, just messes with different energies, desires, emotions. What you become over life depends on how you live, your practices, the relationships you form. You can either just float along not trying, in which case you will end up deadened as a human being, without wisdom. Or you can devote your life to practices of trying to get better. And it’s absolutely a lifelong process. You never get there. But you can train yourself to become better and better by breaking out of your perspective, seeing other possibilities.

    One way of doing this according to the Chinese tradition, which might seem counterintuitive to Western audiences, is ritual. In modern America, we often think that ritual is bad because ritual tells us what to do. Well, in the Chinese tradition, the practices of ritual are about breaking perspective….I discussed this one ritual where a father and a son enter the ritual space. They switch roles. And so each is forced to see the world from the opposite point of view. It’s not that this in itself will give you wisdom, but when you leave the ritual space, you realize, hopefully, the patterns you’ve fallen into, including the dangerous ones, and that opens up more possibilities for changing broken relationships and altering the way we live.

    Is there ever a danger that rituals of this kind become rote themselves and lose their magic and if so, how do you avoid that?

    Michael Puett: Absolutely. The danger is always that our rituals can become conventions that don’t in any way change how we live our lives. We don’t train ourselves through them. The constant work is how do we make our conventions into rituals? It’s always doable, but it is absolutely a conscious project.

    I noticed that most of the different approaches you describe in the book for seeking wisdom ultimately circle back to caring for others and relating well to others. Even Stephanie’s chapter on solitude is ultimately about how solitude can lead to solidarity, which seems pretty counterintuitive. What’s going on here?

    Stephanie Paulsell: I’ve been thinking about this a lot lately. If your other people are your fraternity brothers and Chi Phi at Cornell, some solitude would probably do you some good, give you some space to listen for what you really think is right inside yourself. To explore the subject of solitude, I picked out these three thinkers, Evagrius Ponticus, a fourth century desert monk, the 19th century poet Emily Dickinson, and Howard Thurman, a great African American pastor, theologian, and thinker from the 20th century. For all of them, solitude was really important as a practice of reorientation toward themselves and toward God and toward art making, but above all, toward freedom.

    Evagrius chose solitude because he was trying to get free of his passions, like anger and resentment. If you carry them with you and you try to pray, it’s like pouring water into a bucket with holes in it, he says. If you’re saddening other people, it’s just not going to happen. He started his life as a cosmopolitan guy in Alexandria, and he gradually moves further and further and further out into the desert. And he does become a person who’s understood to be wise, although I think he would say he never got to the end of that quest. 

    Emily Dickinson, of course, engages in a very performative solitude as a woman in the 19th century to be able to write her poetry. She dresses in white and she listens at doors and she does all these things that amaze people. But out of that solitude, she writes 2,000 poems.

    And Howard Thurman was really important to me in the pandemic because he talks about certain forms of emotional contagion. His grandmother was enslaved before the Civil War, but he talks about how her dignity was contagious or how love can be contagious.

    At a time when we were all trying to stay out of each other’s way, he made me think a lot about what you would want to put in circulation and how to do that, like a virus. He lived almost the entire 20th century, so through the worst of Jim Crow, a lot of white terrorist violence against his community. And for him, solitude was not some elite practice for people with a lot of time and leisure on their hands to enjoy. For him, it was a matter of life and death. Without that rich inner life that really belongs to you, you become much more vulnerable to the manipulations of power, he says, when other people try to tell you who you are.

    Thurman said everyone has inside of them an inland sea, and on that sea is an island, and on that island is an altar, and you have to protect it with everything you have, and set an angel with a flaming sword in front of it. He’s very clear about solitude as a double-edged sword. It can be a very damaging practice when you’re put into prison and put into solitary, that’s a very cruel punishment, tantamount to torture. The solitude that was enforced through segregation, he writes a whole book about that. He’s very clear on how damaging solitude can be when it's forced on us. But he also says, “Without the practice of solitude as a humanizing practice, we won’t be able to feel the shape of our own minds, and we won’t learn to hear the sound of the genuine in ourselves, without which we can't hear the sound of the genuine in others.”

    In the final section, there is an interview with Indologist and scholar of Sanskrit and Indian textual traditions Wendy Doniger, who explains why she so badly wanted to go to India when she was a young woman. She just felt it was much wiser than New York City where she was at the time. What do you all think about that? Are some cultures wiser than others? 

    Arthur Kleinman: I would say no culture in any kind of objective sense is wiser than another one. There’d be no basis for saying it. But at certain moments in one’s life, one will be more moved, more inspired, and more challenged in a powerful way by different traditions that might have arisen in various cultures. And so part of our goal was to introduce students to a number of these, some of which might not seem very powerful or interesting to a certain student at a certain moment. And yet 10 years from now, it might suddenly become incredibly important.

    What about our current moment though? It does not seem particularly wise.

    Stephanie Paulsell: Every culture that we’ve studied, every culture has wisdom in it, has its particular forms of wisdom, ways of knowing, ways of questing for wisdom. I don’t think they have to be universal for that to be true. But it’s striking just how different our current moment is from a year ago when we gave this book to Harvard University Press, to say nothing of how long it’s been since we originally wrote those essays.

    There are so many more questions to be addressed about how we seek wisdom in this technological moment, in this political moment. I keep going back to Thurman and my own thinking. How do you learn to listen for the sound of the genuine when it’s getting harder and harder to tell what’s genuine? And what’s artificial intelligence and what’s real intelligence?

    Arthur Kleinman: Michael and I are anthropologists, and in anthropology, the idea is that as you travel and do field research, you’re uncovering difference. You're uncovering the different ways there are of being human. One of the weaknesses in my view of anthropology has been that earlier anthropologists also asked the question, “What do we find when we go into ethnography that are human universals?” But I do think that some kind of quest involving serious concern for meaning in life is a universal phenomenon. I don’t know of an ethnography that has not in some way implied that if not said it directly. That’s closely tied, obviously, to religion, and it’s also closely tied to moral life, and it’s closely tied to aesthetics. And those were the domains we tried to evoke in the book. It’s not just a quest of reading classical texts, but that certainly is important.

    There’s a wonderful book called The Innocent Anthropologist, by a fellow in the 1960s who really wants to be an authentic anthropologist, and he is doing work in Burkina Faso. He gets a toothache and he says to the local people, “I got this terrible toothache.” And they said, “Yeah, let’s take you to the city where there’s a dentist to take care of you.” He said, “No, no, I'm an anthropologist. I want to do it the way you do it here. And they said, “Well, you know, if we have the resources, we go to the city.” “No,” he said, “How do you do it here?” The villager said, “Well, there is a healer down there, but be careful with him."” And so he said, “That's where I'm going. I'm going to the local healer.” And he goes to the local healer, and as he comes up, the local healer knocks him to the ground and takes the pliers and pulls out his two front teeth.

    This is a true story. This is a story about a problem in anthropological knowledge that’s also a problem that we had to confront when we wrote the book, which is the romance of wisdom, the idea that there’s an authentic wisdom that lies in people who are quote unquote “authentic” to their tradition. And that you can get this wisdom and it will magically change things.

    You can be sure this anthropologist who wrote The Innocent Anthropologist never again went to an indigenous shaman to treat his toothache.

    I wanted to make sure, at least in my writing, that I wasn’t simplistic and romantic about wisdom, because I think there is a lot of that in American society. We have the most simplistic understanding of the self. So we wanted to encourage transformation, but not a romanticized, magical version of it: here’s the herb, there's the mushroom, and now you’re wise. No, it’s a practice.

    Is wisdom a purely human thing or is AI, which has been trained on all of humanity’s literature and philosophy, capable of wisdom?

    Stephanie Paulsell: I think our understanding of wisdom is something humanizing and humanistic—and human. There’s wisdom in nature. There’s wisdom in non-human things. But I think the wisdom that we’re very concerned with are practices that support and elevate human dignity. AI can say some smart things to you, for sure. And maybe it’ll cure cancer. That will be fantastic. Maybe it will destroy itself. Maybe the robots will kill us and that won’t be so great. 

    Michael Puett writes in the book that we live in the world of Han Feizi today, a world governed by extraordinarily strong institutions and technologies that leaves us stuck in rote patterns, and yet with a population fully convinced that they live in a world of ­ free individuals. But you also write in the introduction that the quest for wisdom is almost always undertaken in the face of real threat. Could the existential threat AI poses to humanity potentially shake us out of our oblivion, and accelerate a collective quest for wisdom?

    Michael Puett: I would say that one of the dangers when we were writing this book, looking back a few years, was complacency. The sense that things are working out fine, that we didn’t need to worry too much. The attitude was that we had a system that generally worked. Sure, there was climate change, but our system would develop some people who could figure it out.

    That is certainly not the case now. I don’t think people are complacent. And getting to your point, there absolutely is a sense of a crisis. Now, is this good? Is this bad? It depends how we respond. One can respond to a crisis by turning to authoritarian popular leaders who will give seemingly easy answers by scapegoating peoples and claiming that’s the response.

    Sadly, that has proved historically to be a very successful response to crises in the sense of getting a great deal of popular support with obviously just horrific implications. Have there been moments in world history where it’s been the opposite, where a crisis has led to a rethinking of everything in incredibly opening and powerful ways? I'm happy to say the answer is absolutely yes to that. How are we going to respond right now to the current crisis? I think it is completely unpredictable. I don't think complacency is the problem. But I do think the problem now is how do we respond to this crisis? So far, not all of us are doing a great job. But, needless to say, there’s still hope. And one of the many reasons that we hope this book will have an impact is we would like to think some of these wisdom traditions from throughout history might open up possibilities that would otherwise be lost.

And this course slowed me down a little bit, helped me move a little more deliberately. I’m grateful for that.

But part of it, too, is watching how Arthur, Stephanie, and David live their lives, as human beings—the seemingly mundane things, the ways they’ve all wrestled with incredibly difficult moments.

    Arthur Kleinman: I can point to one very practical thing that I came to terms with during the course, and hadn’t had a full understanding of before. And that’s the caring of memories, that’s what I’m working on now. I’ve come to realize that forgetting can actually be, at some points, a wise way of caring for memories. Through the course and through what was happening to me at the time, I came to understand a concept that psychologists call distributed memory.     • Hunting is for Girls—at Least Among Bonobos

    2026-10-28 18:00:00

    by: Jake Currie

    Bonobos have a well-earned reputation as the hippies of the great apes. Not only do they practice free love, using sex to bond socially, the more matriarchal species are also generally more cooperative and less violent than their patriarchal chimpanzee relatives. 

    These different evolutionary pathways were most likely carved by the Congo River around 1.5 to 2 million years ago and are rooted in their diets. Unlike bonobos, chimps north of the river shared their territory with gorillas and competed with them for fruit, forcing them to adopt an omnivorous diet that relied on hunting. Their close relatives south of the river lived in a gorilla-free territory, allowing female bonobos to form social foraging groups that gathered fruit. But they do still hunt, and according to a new study published in Current Biology, females shoulder most of that burden.

    Read more: “How Female Bonobos Rule”

    Primatologists from the Max Planck Institute of Animal Behavior followed two communities of bonobos at the LuiKotale field site in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, cataloging 415 prey captures during the decade-long study period. The team only analyzed captures in which the hunter could be identified, ignoring the odd bonobos who showed up with mystery meat of unknown origins. 

    They found that female hunters accounted for 60 percent of successful captures. “They were the initiators of the hunts,” study author Mattia Bessone of the University of Konstanz in Germany said in a statement. “They were not opportunistically stealing prey from males.”

    Remarkably, motherhood didn’t seem to dissuade the bonobos from hunting—even while they were carrying their infants. The most dangerous game—arboreal monkeys capable of fighting back, like the red colobus—were hunted by male and female bonobos equally. “Young females tear through the treetops to chase monkeys who fight back in defense,” Bessone said. “This is a seriously dangerous business.”

    Chasing down squirrelly quarry like the colobus did seem to be a younger bonobo’s game, however. The team found that older females tended to stick closer to the ground, hunting down small ungulates like antelopes. And they particularly excelled at downing terrestrial prey, accounting for three times as many successful hunts as their male counterparts. 

    This unequal division of labor helped cement a dominant role for females, like it does for male chimpanzees. Once they felled their prey they were also able to keep it for themselves, deciding who got to share in the bounty. “Catching prey is one thing but maintaining control of the carcass is another one,” study author Gottfried Hohmann of the Max Planck Institute explained. “It’s possible that males are not capturing large prey in order to avoid conflicts with females over meat.”

    In the bonobo world, some women really can have it all.

    • When Frog Eyes Are Like Fingerprints

    2026-10-28 16:00:00

    by: Devin Reese

    We have identification of humans down pat—a complete human fingerprint can pinpoint the person it belongs to more than 90 percent of the time, according to forensic scientists. To study animals in the wild, biologists also need to identify individuals, but they don’t have reliable proxies for fingerprints. That’s because, other than mammals, vertebrate animals lack unique ridge patterns on their fingers.

    But now, a study published in the journal Herpetozoa reports a reliable way to identify individual frogs without having to catch them. Typically, frog tissue samples for DNA analysis are clipped from the ends of toes. The capture and clipping can be stressful, however, especially for tree frogs, which use their toes to stick onto substrates. 

    Read more: “The Secret Lives of Japanese Temple Frogs”

    “Personally, I was uncomfortable with this approach, especially given that these are arboreal frogs that rely on their limbs to climb trees,” explained lead study author Mélissa Peignier from the University of Bern in Switzerland. 

    And so, having noticed the unusual eyes of the egg-eating spiny-backed frog (Osteocephalus oophagus)—its irises are golden colored with radial black lines like the spokes of a wheel—Peignier and her coauthors decided to test whether such iris patterns were distinct enough for individual recognition. 

    They captured and released frogs over several years in lowland rainforests of French Guiana, a stronghold for spiny-backed frogs, which stay in the forest canopy during the day but descend to ponds for breeding during the rainy season. Each frog was hand-caught, then photographed and released. Photos of their eyes were compiled into a reference library for repeat use in identification.

    Of the 392 frogs captured, 103 unique individuals were identified by their iris patterns. And within a season, more than half of these individuals were recaptured and consistently reidentified from their irises, showing that iris color patterns are stable at least in the short run—and a much more humane way to potentially track such species in the future. 

    Or as Peignier put it, “If we can reliably identify individuals using naturally occurring features, we may be able to reduce the need for more invasive marking techniques while still collecting the data needed to answer important scientific questions.”
