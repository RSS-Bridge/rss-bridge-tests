How You Squeeze Planetary Minerals Changes Everything

By Laurence Tognetti, MSc - October 30, 2026 02:28 AM UTC | Planetary Science - 2.4K reads

Studying the interiors of planets is essential for understanding planetary formation, evolution, and their ability to potentially host life as we know it. To accomplish this, researchers use a combination of Earth as an analog, computer models, meteorites, and data collected from other planetary bodies for studying their interior compositions. As a result, scientists have determined that the elements of magnesium and silicon are the most abundant planetary building blocks. And, under extreme pressures like the interior mantle of our planet, it forms magnesium oxide (MgO) crystals. But how does this same process occur under different pressures and temperatures on other planets like gas giants and larger rocky exoplanets called super-Earths?

Now, a collaborative team of researchers from the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) and Johns Hopkins University might be one step closer to solving this conundrum. As discussed in a recent study published in Physical Review X, the researchers unveiled intriguing findings that could solve a 12-year investigation regarding the geochemical processes that occur deep inside planets, specifically related to the formation of magnesium oxide. This study is a third study in a series that builds off two previous studies, a 2015 study published in Physical Review Letters and a 2024 study published in Science Advances.

Artist's impression of the interior layers of Earth, Mars and the Moon. (Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech)

For the study, the researchers conducted a series of laboratory experiments using lasers on a cubic crystal structure of magnesium oxide to compress and simulate the extreme temperatures and pressures it experiences in planetary interiors. For example, the researchers tested as high as 572 Gigapascals (GPa) and 13,000 Kelvin (K) (12,927 degrees Celsius/23,300 degrees Fahrenheit), which are much higher than the pressures and temperatures near the center of the Earth at about 360 GPa and 6,000 K (5,727 degrees Celsius/10,340 degrees Fahrenheit), respectively.

The laser compressed the MgO cube from various directions, which challenged longstanding scientific theories that the extreme temperatures and pressures made the direction of compression irrelevant. The three directions included diagonally along the face, perpendicular to a cube face, and along the diagonal through the face.

Credit: NASA/Lunar and Planetary Institute

In the end, the researchers found that the direction of the incoming laser onto the MgO cube resulted in different responses regarding its compression. For example, while the cube’s transformation along the diagonal through the cube, whereas the other two directions resulted in different responses, specifically disordered and irregular crystal growth behind a shock wave from the compression. While this finding refuted longstanding theories regarding the behavior of crystals based on direction, the researchers also found that the MgO cube melted at 572 GPa and 13,000 K, which set a new experimental known material record for pressure and temperature.

“Magnesium oxide is an important ingredient in our understanding of planetary interiors,” said Dr. June Wicks, who is a professor in Johns Hopkins’ Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences and a co-author on the study. “These experiments help us determine where it can remain solid and where it becomes liquid, which matters for how planets transport heat and evolve.”

As noted, magnesium and silicon comprise the most abundant elements in planetary interiors, with its combined form of MgO revealing the temperatures and pressures of those same planetary interiors. The three main geologic layers of a rock planetary object are the crust, mantle, and core, with the Earth having a liquid outer core and a rocky inner core, with MgO forming near the bottom of the mantle near the core-mantle boundary. However, this process is different depending on the type of planet.

Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech

For example, on super-Earth exoplanets, MgO can transform into a conductive liquid within the mantle (whereas this conductivity exists within the Earth’s outer core), resulting in magnetic fields much stronger than Earth’s. Additionally, MgO on gas giants like Jupiter and Saturn can exist either as a solid or liquid deep within their cores and depending on the temperatures.

What new insights into planetary interior processes will researchers make in the coming years and decades? Only time will tell, and this is why we science!

It's a Ghost! No, It's a Stellar Crèche!

2026-10-29 23:25:37

by: Carolyn Collins Petersen (https://www.universetoday.com/authors/cc-petersen)

It's a Ghost! No, It's a Stellar Crèche!

By Carolyn Collins Petersen - October 29, 2026 11:25 PM UTC | Stars - 2.6K reads

Astronomers using the Chandra X-ray Observatory (CXO) and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) zeroed in on a wispy-looking region of the Carina Nebula and found a ghostly looking cloud seemingly floating in isolation among the other clouds of gas and dust. Just in time for Halloween, although, it turns out that little cloud isn't the soul of a departed star. Instead, it's a region of the nebula that has been actively engaged in creating batches of hot, young new stars.

By studying this region in X-rays and infrared light, astronomers are getting a good look at the structure of the cloud as well as the stars it has formed. Not only that, but it helps them piece together more information about the larger Carina Nebula, which lies more than 7,500 light-years away from us.

Most of us are familiar with one of the brilliant and weird objects that lights up the nebula: Eta Carinae. It looks like one star, but is actually a binary that has a combined brightness of around 5 million times that of our Sun. Periodically, this object brightens and dims, probably due to clouds of condensed dust that coalesced around the binary stars. It experienced a huge outburst in the 1840s called the "Great Eruption", which boosted tremendous amounts of gas and dust into space. It has since experienced other, smaller outbursts.

An ultraviolet image of the Homunculus Nebula (Eta Carinae) taken by the Hubble Telescope. Credit: NASA/ESA/StSci

Visiting the Carina Nebula

The Carina complex is a huge diffuse nebula containing a collection of newborn stars nestled in a sea of starmaking gas and dust. It continues to produce new stars, many of them extremely bright and massive. Many telescopes have studied it, most notably the Hubble Space Telescope and JWST. Each view brings new insights into the makeup of the cloud as well as the objects it contains. In addition, astronomers are also keeping an eye on Eta Carinae because its primary star — a luminous blue variable star &emdash; is likely to explode as a supernova. When that happens, the shock waves will transform much of the nebula, probably spurring new rounds of star birth.

A broad panorama of the Carina Nebula, a region of massive star formation in the southern skies. This new method of determining the age of stars will help astronomers better understand the process of star formation. Credit: ESO/T. Preibisch

Obviously, the Eta Carina binary is a tantalizing subject, but there are also places such as the Keyhole Nebula. It's a dark and cold molecular cloud that covers about 7 light-years of space. Ionizing radiation from the nearby Eta Carina binary is shaping and eating away at the Keyhole.

There's also the open cluster Trumpler 14, which is a six light-year-wide grouping of more than 2,000 OB-type stars. These are massive and short-lived stars and are very bright in ultraviolet light. These are very young stars, ranging in age from about 300,000 to 500,000 years old.

The ghostly nebula that CXO and JWST imaged is still making stars at its heart. At the same time, ionizing radiation from the nearby Eta Carina binary, as well as hot young stars, is eating away the materials in the cloud. Thanks to that "cannibalizing" ultraviolet radiation from those stars, the cloud has developed several comet-like "knots". Astronomers often refer to them as cometary globules because of their familiar shapes. The Carina Nebula is not the only place where such cometary knots are seen. Astronomers have spotted them in other star-forming regions as well as in planetary nebulae, where stellar winds collide with material previously ejected from a dying star like the Sun. As those collisions happen, they shape similar types of knots in the clouds of gas and dust.

Since the stars will have much shorter lifetimes than that of the Sun, someday this region will look more like a stellar graveyard. The remains of those stars we see as newborn today will, in tens of millions of years, be ghosts of their former selves, spreading their materials to interstellar space.

Examining the Cat's Head Nebula with the Hubble and JWST

2026-10-29 22:38:52

by: Evan Gough (https://www.universetoday.com/authors/ion23drive)

Examining the Cat's Head Nebula with the Hubble and JWST

By Evan Gough - October 29, 2026 10:38 PM UTC | Observing - 3.4K reads

The Cat's Head nebula gets its name from its appearance: it looks like a cat sitting so that it's facing away from the viewer, with its head turned around on the top of its neck, highlighted by a star that looks like a glowing eye. The unusual shape is made of gas and dust carved and formed by the radiation of young stars.

It's a small part of the larger Carina Nebula, a complex star-forming region about 7,500 light years away. Recently, both the Hubble and the JWST cast their gaze on the Cat's Head in an effort to learn more about star formation, a constant research subject in astronomy.

The Carina Nebula hosts some of the most massive stars in the entire Milky Way in its Carina OB1 assocation. Of particular interest is Eta Carinae, a stellar system that hosts many stars, two of which have a combined luminosity more than 5 million times greater than the Sun. Eta Carinae is also known for being at the heart of the eerily creepy Homunculous Nebula, not visible in these images. Massive stars like the ones in Eta Carinae and their powerful radiation have a pronounced effect on the region, and on star formation itself.

https://www.youtube.com/embed/RNBoibbQ9Vk

Hubble's image of the Cat's Head is in visible light, while the JWST imaged it in both near and mid-infrared. Each of the images shows a different aspect of the Cat's Head.

The pillar that makes the Cat's Head is composed of thick, dark dust, so far resisting the onslaught of stellar wind and radiation from massive stars. The yellow outline on its right side is likely where photoevaporation is taking place, driven by the same powerful stars. The UV radiation is ionizing the gas on the rim, and will eventually evaporate it completely.

In astronomy images like these, different filters isolate different chemicals like sulphur, oxygen, and hydrogen. The ionization level of the chemicals, along with how highly they're energized and how much dust is present, all affect the colours. Whether we know which colours represent which chemicals or not, these images are awe-inspiring.

The massive stars in the region are also responsible for the arcs and shoals in the images, again caused by their powerful radiation and stellar winds.

This zoomed-in portion of the Hubble's image highlights the "eyeball", likely a star in just the right position. Image Credit: NASA, ESA, STScI. Image Processing: J. Depasquale (STScI).

The JWST, as an infrared telescope, presents different, yet no less gorgeous, images of the Cat's Head. It's infrared instruments see things differently than the Hubble does. This gives the Cat's Head—already ethereal in the Hubble image—an even more ghostly, almost unreal appearance. Some parts of the Cat's Head in the JWST images appear to be only half-there, with background stars shining brightly through them.

The JWST's near-infrared image doesn't show different chemical elements the same way the Hubble image does. Instead, it shows more of the stars themselves. Even background galaxies are visible beyond the Cat's Head, along with the black background of space. The JWST also shows how massive stars are shaping the region, with the wispy filaments on the images left side. Image Credit: NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI. Image Processing: J. Depasquale (STScI)

Some of the turbulence in the gas, driven by the massive stars, appears more substantial in the JWST image. The star that makes the Cat's Eye is also brighter, with less of its light blocked by dust in infrared.

A zoomed-in JWST NIR image of the head in the Cat's Head Nebula. Image Credit: NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI. Image Processing: J. Depasquale (STScI)

In the JWST's mid-infrared image, the Cat's Head Nebula is transformed yet again. It's muted and ghost-sheet white, surrounded by pastel gas clouds punctuated with stars. The clouds look almost Earth-like in their white colour. This time, it's carbon molecules and warm dust emissions that take the stage. What is most likely an infant star pops into view in this image, embedded in thick dust that it's yet to dissipate, to the right of the head itself.

The white colour of the gas in this mid-infrared JWST image makes it look like Earthly clouds. Fewer stars are visible in the background, their light blocked out by the gas, which appears thicker. In the gas to the left, finer detail is visible in the gas clouds. Image Credit: NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI. Image Processing: J. Depasquale (STScI).

The head itself is muted in the MIR image, along with the rest of the nebula. It's eye, almost malevolent in the other images, blends in with the gas and is less striking.

In this zoom-in of the MIR image, the edges are softened, and so are the colours. The star that makes they eye blends in with the gas in gradients of white. Globules below the head look like they're reaching for the head, rather than being blown back by stellar wind and radiation. The blackness of space is visible in the upper right, and a little in the upper left. Image Credit: NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI. Image Processing: J. Depasquale (STScI).

Though these images are easy to appreciate by almost anyone, their primary purpose is scientific. By emphasizing and de-emphasizing different aspects of the Cat's Head nebula, astronomers can see stars more clearly, along with the dust, the gas, and the chemistry. Together, they help researchers see the effects of stellar radiation and wind, called stellar feedback, on the ongoing process of star formation.

Stellar feedback can both encourage and stifle star formation, depending on circumstances. It's complicated, and it's an ongoing topic of astronomical study. One of the JWST's main, defining science themes is star formation, and images like these are one of the reasons it was built. The Hubble helps too, and illustrates how the gas, the dust, the metallicity, and the stars of different sizes and ages, all play a role. Those factors all interact in different ways, making it difficult to come up with concrete theories of how it all works.

These gorgeous images are more than eye candy to astronomers. They're working hard on understanding a process fundamental to the cosmos. Wrapped up in all this visual chaos and beauty, Nature's laws are defining how stars form. Astronomers' mission is to decipher it all.

Enjoying these images on their visual merit alone is our mission, if we accept it.

A Brief-ish History of METI. Part II: From Interplanetary to Interstellar

2026-10-29 20:22:10

by: Matthew Williams (https://www.universetoday.com/authors/houseofwilliams)

A Brief-ish History of METI. Part II: From Interplanetary to Interstellar

By Matthew Williams - October 29, 2026 08:22 PM UTC | Space Exploration - 3K reads

Welcome back to our ongoing series on the Brief-ish History of METI, where we examine the rise, development, and possible implications of Messaging Extraterrestrial Intelligence. In our first installment, we explored some of the foundational principles and how METI emerged as a possible answer to the "SETI Paradox." In a Universe where everyone is listening, and no one is broadcasting, should we as a species take the first step?

Naturally, this subject has sparked controversy and heated debate. In a Universe so vast and filled with so many unknowns and possibilities, is it wise to announce ourselves to potential alien civilizations? If we assume only 1 in 100 are hostile, what would be the consequences of sending a message saying, "Here we are!"? On the other hand, if we choose to remain silent and only listen to the cosmos, could we be missing out? By imposing isolation on ourselves, might we be damning ourselves to extinction?

Alas, the debate over the ethics of METI is far from over and will likely continue until (dare we say it?) we make contact someday. But that's a discussion for another installment. In the meantime, we'll continue our examination of the important milestones that led to METI as a distinct field of study. And in the midst of all that, we will be looking at the luminaries who were chiefly responsible for advancing it.

The Great Leap

By the latter half of the 20th century, SETI shifted from a relatively obscure pursuit carried out largely by independently wealthy individuals to a serious scientific endeavor. With advances in rocketry and spaceflight, and the launch of national space programs, interest in the possibility of extraterrestrial life surged again. Rather than being fueled by science fiction and scientific inaccuracies (the misinterpretation of Schiaparelli's "canali"), the fascination was fueled by questions of what humanity might find on other worlds.

Cornell Professor and SETI pioneer Frank Drake standing in front of the 25-meter Howard E. Tatel Radio Telescope at the Green Bank Observatory. Credit: NRAO/AUI/NSF

This, in turn, led to experiments designed to test the capabilities of modern technology and perhaps make contact with other intelligent beings. A prime example is The Morse Message, which scientists at the Evpatoria Planetary Radar (EPR) center in Crimea carried out between November 19th and 24th, 1962. Using the facility's RT-70 radio telescope, the science team transmitted a Morse Code signal toward Venus. The message included the letters M-I-R (Mir, the Russian word for "peace") followed by "Lenin" and "USSR."

The message's main purpose was not to contact an intelligent civilization, but to test the radar station. The signals reflected off Venus's surface and were received back on Earth about four and a half minutes after transmission. Nevertheless, this experiment occured at a time when Venus was still thought to be potentially habitable, with many scientists believing that Venus' dense cloud cover (which obscured its surface) was due to heavy precipitation.

A few months later (December 14th, 1962), the Mariner 4 probe made the first detailed measurements of the planet's surface temperature. In the years that followed, multiple Soviet probes and landers revealed the true extent of Venus' hellish conditions: a carbon dioxide atmosphere, sulfuric-acid rain, and the hottest surface temperatures in the Solar System. This forever dispelled the idea that Venus could be a watery planet covered in bogs and oceans.

More importantly, it was also during this time that a transition occured in METI, reflecting a similar transition with SETI. Rather than listening or messaging neighboring planets for signs of life (on behalf of one nation), efforts in the latter half of the 20th century would reach interstellar space. Between 1999 and 2015, the EPR center played a vital role in METI by providing the necessary communications infrastructure for multiple publicly funded experiments.

While the Cold War Era added impetus to SETI and METI efforts, with both superpowers seeing a strategic advantage in making contact first, the scientific motivation reflected a broader, more universal awareness, with messages sent on behalf of all humanity.

Frank Drake

When it comes to the pioneering work that made the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence (SETI) a legitimate scientific field, few people were more integral than Frank Drake. Born in Chicago in 1930, Drake showed an early interest in science and later pursued a degree in astronomy at Cornell University. His interest in extraterrestrial life was reinforced by a 1951 lecture by Russian-American astronomer Otto Struve. One of the most distinguished and prolific astronomers of the mid-20th century, Struve was an early SETI advocate and greatly raised its international prestige.

After receiving a B.A. in Engineering Physics, Drake served briefly in the Naval Reserve before attending graduate school at Harvard University. By 1955, he received an M.S. and Ph.D. in astronomy. From 1958 to 1963, Drake began his career as a radio astronomer with the National Radio Astronomy Observatory (NRAO) in Green Bank, West Virginia. There, he studied radio emissions from the Solar System's planets using the GBT, as well as those coming from the center of the Milky Way.

Drake was also deeply involved in the development of the Arecibo Observatory, which began construction in 1960, and helped extend its capabilities to include radio astronomy. Originally, the observatory was designed for ionosphere studies and to help track incoming missiles in the event of nuclear war. A year before Arecibo began construction, Drake obtained the approval of Otto Struve (then director of the NRAO) to begin humanity's first true SETI experiment.

The project began observations in 1960, using the NRAO 26-meter radio telescope at the Greenbank Observatory in West Virginia to listen for possible signals from Tau Ceti and Epsilon Eridani - two Sun-like stars located 12 and 10.5 light-years from Earth (respectively). While the project did not detect any extraterrestrial signals, it garnered international attention and drew the attention of many prominent scientists. This included Carl Sagan, who met Drake a year later at the first SETI symposium, and the two began a lifelong collaboration.

It was at this symposium, held at the Greenbank Observatory, that Drake unveiled his famous equation for estimating the number of extraterrestrial civilizations (ETCs) that humanity could communicate with in our galaxy at any given time: The Drake Equation. Rather than a scientific equation or method for calculating the existence of ETI, Drake created it as a thought experiment to summarize the challenges SETI researchers face.

In essence, knowing the number of civilizations (N) meant knowing all the parameters, the most important of which was how long civilizations could broadcast before going extinct (L). At the time, scientists knew that all of these parameters, except for the first (the average rate of star formation in the Milky Way), were subject to immense uncertainties. Nevertheless, the equation remains foundational to SETI and is considered the second-best known equation in human history (the first being Einstein's equation for Special Relativity (E=mc2)

Illustration of the Drake Equation (Credit: NOIRLab/AURA/NSF/P. Marenfeld)

The Arecibo Message

After completing what was arguably humanity's first true SETI experiment, Drake began work on what would become humanity's first true METI effort: The Arecibo Message. Work began in the early 1970s, as Frank Drake organized a campaign to compose a message to be broadcast via Arecibo's megawatt transmitter attached to its 305-meter (1000-foot) antenna.

The message was transmitted on November 16th, 1974, at 2380 MHz with an effective bandwidth of 10 Hz and a raw carrier power of 450 kilowatts (kW). However, Arecibo's huge dish focused that energy into an incredibly narrow, directional beam that magnified its intensity exponentially, making it equivalent to a 20-gigawatt omnidirectional broadcast. This meant any radio antenna in the galaxy could detect the message, provided it was about the same size as Arecibo.

The total broadcast lasted less than three minutes and was directed at Messier 13 (M13), a globular star cluster located about 25,000 light-years from Earth. This cluster contains 300,000 to 500,000 stars and spans 145 to 165 light-years in diameter. Because of the number of stars and the cluster's age (an estimated 11.65 billion years), it was considered a likely place for an extraterrestrial civilization.

Drake composed the message with help from Carl Sagan and other prominent astronomers, who arranged it using prime numbers because they believed this would make it easier for an ETC to decode. It consisted of a 1679-binary-digit picture (210 bytes), the product of two prime numbers, arranged rectangularly into 73 lines of 23 characters per line (also prime numbers). The binary digits (ones and zeroes) were transmitted via frequency shifting at 10 bits per second.

The decoded message was a simple pictograph depicting a series of scientific, geographical, biological, and astronomical information in different colors. These included:

  • A counting scheme of 1 to 10 (white)
  • The atomic numbers for hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and phosphorus, which make up DNA (purple)
  • The chemical formula of the four purine and pyrimidine bases that make up DNA (green)
  • An image of the DNA double helix and an estimate of the number of nucleotides (blue and white, respectively)
  • A stick-figure of a human being (red), our average dimensions (blue/white), and the human population of Earth (white)
  • A depiction of the Solar System, indicating that the message is coming from the third planet (yellow)
  • A schematic of the Arecibo Observatory and its dimensions (purple/white and blue)

The legacy of the Arecibo Message is comparable only to that of Project Ozma, as both experiments are recognized as humanity's first true attempts to find ETI. The message also became the subject of a hoax in 2001, known as the "Arecibo Answer." Near the Chilbolton telescope in Hampshire, UK, an imprint was found in a crop field that was a near-replica of the original message, using the same 73 x 23 grid pattern and most of the same chemical data.

However, the section on the elements of DNA substituted silicon for carbon, implying silicon-based life, and the DNA diagram was rewritten. The image of a human body was also replaced with a figure with a bulbous head and enlarged eyes. A system of nine planets was also depicted, but with emphasis placed on the 3rd, 4th, and 5th planets. The Arecibo telescope was replaced with a replica of a crop circle found in the same field a year earlier, and the transmitter's diameter was altered.

These align with common science fiction tropes about silicon-based extraterrestrials and urban myths about "greys" or "little green men." The medium, a burnt crop circle, is also a common hoax technique, meant to resemble burn marks from a landing craft. The SETI Institute issued a statement saying that the crop formation was a hoax, undoubtedly perpetrated by the same people who had made crop circles in the same area during the previous two years.

"The crop circles are decorative, impressive, and provocative, but not informative," they wrote. "We can expect better from true extraterrestrial intelligence."

By the 1970s, SETI and METI efforts had matured considerably, with developments in one field mirroring those in the other. Beyond the Arecibo Message, this decade saw the first (and, to date, greatest) attempts to send physical messages into space, a collaborative effort led by Frank Drake and Carl Sagan: the Pioneer Plaques and Voyager Golden Records. No Comets Required? How the Growing Earth May Have Created Its Own Water

2026-10-29 18:25:52

by: Evan Gough (https://www.universetoday.com/authors/ion23drive)

No Comets Required? How the Growing Earth May Have Created Its Own Water

By Evan Gough - October 29, 2026 06:25 PM UTC | Planetary Science - 3.5K reads

The origin of Earth's water is one of those foundational issues that explains our planet's habitability. The idea that water was delivered by comets and asteroids has been on the minds of planetary scientists for a long time. Both types of bodies, and especially comets, are known to have water, and both types are known to have crashed into Earth in large numbers during the planet's early ages.

But there's also a growing understanding that rocky planets form with a primordial envelope rich in hydrogen and helium that they acquire from their solar nebulae. Since water is—obviously—H2O, then Earth was born with substantial amounts of hydrogen, and all that's required was oxygen. Did that need to be delivered to Earth somehow?

Sort of, but not exactly, and new research shows why. It's titled "Dry pebble rain could have delivered water to Earth through hydrogen reduction of iron oxide," and it's published in Nature Geoscience. The lead authors are Susmita Garai, a graduate student in Earth and Planetary Sciences at the University of New Mexico, and Zachary Sharp, professor and director of Earth and Planetary Sciences at UNM.

"Looking out over a large body of water, it is hard to imagine that that water may have been produced 4 ½ billion years ago in a hell-like atmosphere, but it very well may have.”- Zachary Sharp, University of New Mexico.

"Water is vital to planet habitability. However, the sources of water for terrestrial planets and timing of delivery remain controversial," the authors write. There are timeline constraints for how this water could have been delivered by comets and asteriods. It has to take into account the giant impact that created the Moon, the Late Heavy Bombardment, and the abundance of certain elements in the Earth's mantle. It's difficult to fit delivery by comets and asteroids in with these constraints.

This illustration shows a young planet in its magma ocean phase. Terrestrial planets like Earth were entirely molten when they formed. It's possible that water was delivered to young planets by comets and asteroids during the magma ocean phase. Image Credit: Merikanto via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0 https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Volcanic_planet_4_r_1.png

If Earth's water didn't come from an external source and was generated locally, then it sidesteps these constraints. "Here we show experimentally how oceans of water can be produced within the high-temperature H2-rich atmosphere above a terrestrial planet growing by capture of millimetre-to-centimetre-size silicate pebbles," the authors write.

“Previous ideas about the source of water to Earth rely on an external source, such as comets or meteorites. Here we show that virtually any Earth-sized planet should have water by this newly identified mechanism,” co-author Sharp explained in a press release. “In order to evaluate the possibility of ‘pebble’ rain’ being a source of water, we needed to construct and execute some very challenging high temperature experiments. Dr. Garai’s efforts paid off mightily, as she found that water is produced in just minutes in the high temperature environment of the Early Earth’s atmosphere.”

When a protoplanet's mass exceeds about 0.3 Earth masses, chondrules and pebbles fall through the atmosphere slowly. For hours or even days, these pebbles are above their melting point and experience extensive FeO reduction. This removes the oxygen and releases it into the atmosphere to form water, leaving metallic iron. For this to happen, there needs to be a powerful reducing agent at high temperatures, and in this case, it's the heated hydrogen in the atmosphere.

"The gravitational potential energy released by settling pebbles directly heats the atmosphere, resulting in a steep atmospheric thermal gradient, and for large planets, very high temperatures at the surface." These temperatures can exceed 3,000 K (1650 C) and the pressure can exceed 200 bars (~197 sea-level pressure). A magma ocean also heats everything up from below.

"Together, these processes result in molten pebble rain onto the protoplanet surface, volatile loss from pebbles in the atmosphere and ingassing of hydrogen, helium and other volatiles into the magma ocean," the researchers write.

This paints a dramatic picture unlike anything a human being has ever witnessed. At the heart of it is a very young Earth in its magma ocean phase. It's a ball of molten rock, to which an atmosphere nothing like the modern atmosphere clings. Falling through that atmosphere is a rain of molten pebbles, material that's coalesced out of the solar nebula, drawn by Earth's increasing mass to accrete onto the planet's hellish surface.

This figures show how molten pebbles rained onto Earth's surface, falling through the atmosphere and off-gassing volatiles, hydrogen and helium among them. Some of the hydrogen and helium escapes, and some in-gasses into the magma ocean. Image Credit: Garai et al. 2026. NatGeo

The researchers tested this scenario by running some high-temperature laboratory experiments that came as close to the hellish conditions in Earth's Hadean eon as possible. "To quantify the amounts and the kinetics of iron reduction in pebbles settling in through a nebular atmosphere, we conducted a series of experiments in which mafic and ultramafic silicates were melted in He and H2 gas streams," the authors explain. Mafic and ultramafic are the two main categories of igneous rocks, both rich in iron and magnesium.

This image illustrates some of the lab work in the study. It shows an experimental apparatus heating silicate rock samples. Garai et al. 2026. NatGeo

The researchers heated silicate rock samples to between 1,200 and 1,400 degrees Celsius in streams of hydrogen, and separately in streams of helium. The experiments lasted between 15 minutes and three hours. The conditions mimicked the time in Earth's growth where it was massive enough to attract a rain of pebbles and was also massive enough to have a hydrogen-rich atmosphere, super-heated and highly pressurized. The moving streams of hydrogen and helium mimicked the pebbles movement through that atmosphere.

The results showed that in the hydrogen, almost 80% of the iron oxide was converted to metallic iron, producing water vapour as a result. In the helium, the same reaction didn't occur.

The efficiency of the reduction depends on the metal fraction in the pebbles, which the researchers calculated. "Partial reduction of pebble FeO at these efficiencies yields prodigious amounts of water," they write. "At peak efficiency, several hundred ocean masses of water are produced from the A composition, and tens of ocean masses from the B composition." The A and B compositions come from previous research.

These panels show how many oceans worth of water is produced by pebbles with with different compositions and different iron concentrations. Image Credit: Garai et al. 2026. NatGeo

"We had suspected and hoped that we would produce iron metal and water in our reduction experiments, but we really had no way of knowing how fast and how much. We were quite surprised to find that extrapolation of our work suggests that hundreds of oceans worth of water were likely produced in this way. Looking out over a large body of water, it is hard to imagine that that water may have been produced 4 ½ billion years ago in a hell-like atmosphere, but it very well may have,” said Sharp.

The idea that Earth's water came from comets and asteroids was always a little shaky, though still compelling. This research shows a different way that Earth could've acquired its life-giving water. The results may also extend to other worlds around other stars, and even to our neighbour Mars.

“The idea of pebble accretion is still relatively new. It’s only been around for the last decade. But it seems to solve many problems that exist with the existing paradigm for planet formation,” Sharp said. “And now we see that it also can explain why our planet is habitable. By extension, it is reasonable to expect that other Earth-sized bodies circling other stars would also have water and possibly life.”

This work is early proof that the basic mechanism is possible. But as for how much of Earth's water actually stemmed from this, that needs more research. "The net water gain depends strongly on the amount of produced water extracted by exhaust to the nebula," the researchers write. "Although subsequent water loss by atmospheric escape is possible, water produced during pebble rain represents an early and fundamental step in the evolution of a habitable planet."

It's still possible, for instance, that other processes also played a role, and even that comets and asteroids did deliver some of our planet's water.

The source of Earth's water will remain an ongoing subject of research. Over time, the picture will become clearer.

Colliding Dwarf Planets Created a 'Cat's Tail' 1,000 Astronomical Units Long

2026-10-29 15:46:02

by: Evan Gough (https://www.universetoday.com/authors/ion23drive)

Colliding Dwarf Planets Created a 'Cat's Tail' 1,000 Astronomical Units Long

By Evan Gough - October 29, 2026 03:46 PM UTC | Planetary Science - 5.6K reads

Beta Pictoris (β Pictoris), a young main sequence star about 63 light years away, is known for having the first directly-imaged debris disk. Three planets in that disk have also been directly-imaged. One of them, Beta Pictoris c, is also the closest exoplanet to its star that has ever been directly imaged.

This is a composite image of of Beta Pictoris as seen in near infrared light. Beta Pictoris B is shown, the closest exoplanet to its star that's ever been directly imaged. Due to its proximity and other factors, it's an object of much observation and research. Image Credit: By ESO, CC BY 4.0, https://commons.wikimedia.org/w/index.php?curid=5268432

β Pictoris is the subject of lots of research because of its youth, its proximity, its resolved debris-disk, and because its geometry makes it open to detailed observations. In particular, telescopes can get high-resolution side-views of its disk's structure, including its warped nature and its asymmetries. Astronomers have even found evidence of exocomets in the system.

In a paper published in 2024, researchers presented the JWST's first images of β Pictoris and its debris disk. "We report for the first time the presence of an extended secondary disk in thermal emission, with a curved extension bent away from the plane of the disk," the authors wrote. They called it a “cat’s tail,” and suggested that it was connected to other features found in the disk: a clump of carbon monoxide, a warp in the disk, and a "mid-infrared asymmetry detected on the southwest side of the disk."

Unusual features like the Cat's Tail, which extends for about 1,000 astronomical units, always pique the interest of researchers, who want to know how it was created. New research led by a University of Arizona scientist has figured out what created the unusual feature. The research is titled "Deep HST/STIS Coronagraphic Imaging of the β Pictoris Debris Disk: On the Scattered Light Component of the Cat’s Tail," and is published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters. The lead author is Arin Avsar, a PhD Candidate in planetary science in the Lunar and Planetary Laboratory at the UofA.

"The β Pictoris debris disk is a unique system where mid-infrared coronagraphic imaging with JWST/Mid-Infrared Imager (MIRI) revealed a new spatially resolved substructure known as the Cat’s Tail," the authors write. "We present deep Hubble Space Telescope (HST)/Space Telescope Imaging Spectrograph (STIS) coronagraphic imaging of β Pic in scattered light," the authors add. Their Hubble observations were obtained in six epochs between 2012 and 2025, which they used to search for the cat's tail's scattered light.

The β Pictoris disk can be seen in scattered light, precisely because it's a dusty disk, and the dust scatters the light. The colour and the brightness of the scattered light reveals the properties of the dust, including the size of its grains, their porosity, and the dust's overall composition. Identifying those properties can explain where the dust came from.

"The Cat's Tail is thought to be the aftermath of a collision between large bodies – think something like Pluto," lead author Avsar said in a press release. "Oddly, this feature had previously only been seen with the James Webb Space Telescope, which detects infrared light, but remained invisible at shorter wavelengths, which has puzzled the community for a while."

"We think the Cat's Tail has eluded our observations in visible light until now because of what it's made of," Avsar explained. "It consists almost entirely of what we call organic refractory material, which is heavily carbon-based. These long chains of carbon atoms absorb visible light, so they're quite black."

These panels show the cat's tail feature (white arrow) in visible light from the Hubble on the left, and in mid-infrared light from the JWST on the right. Image Credit: Arin M. Avsar et al 2026 ApJL 1010 L10

The Cat Tail's composition of organic refractory material is a clue to the objects that collided and created it. Both this research and the 2024 research show that the Cat's Tail is predominantly composed of porous organic refractory material. "In the solar system, organic refractory material best reproduces the red surface spectra of Kuiper Belt objects, most notably Pluto," the authors write. NASA's New Horizons found tholins, a type of refractory organic compound, on Pluto's surface, and on the surface of its companion, Charon. But tholins make up only a small proportion of Pluto's mass, not enough to create the Cat's Tail. "This points to organic material from the interior of the disrupted planetesimals as the likely origin of the organic refractory material seen in the Cat’s Tail."

"This invites the question: do dwarf planets in exo-Kuiper Belts (like that of β Pic) contain enough organic material to create the Cat’s Tail?" the researchers write. To answer that, they examined the amount of organics in the local ISM, which placed constraints in the amount available in the Beta Pictoris disk, where the colliding objects formed. They also examined the amount present in comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko, an icy body visited by the ESA's Rosetta mission.

These measurements let the researchers calculate what the combined mass of the colliding objects must have been, in order to supply enough refractory material needed to create the tail.

"... we find that each parent body must have had a mass of ∼1–3 × 1021 kg to produce 2 × 1019 kg of organic refractory debris in the Cat’s Tail," the authors write. That equates to a few percent of the mass of Earth's Moon, or about 1 to 3 times the mass of the dwarf planet Ceres. These amounts agree with the estimates in the 2024 paper that initially found the Cat's Tail.

This figure helps show how the JWST and the Hubble see things differently, and how their combined observations have a powerful symmetry. The top panel is an RGB image of the HST/STIS image presented in this work overlaid with the deconvolved F1550C image from the 2024 work. The bottom two images show a grayscale version of each dataset in the RGB image. They highlight the smooth/axisymmetric halo in the HST/STIS image in contrast to the complex substructures seen in the F1550C image. Image Credit: Arin M. Avsar et al 2026 ApJL 1010 L10

The researchers also calculated the sizes of the objects that collided. Assuming that they had bulk densities in line with the dwarf planets in our Solar System, the objects must have had radii between about 600km and 800 km. "The mass and radius range is similar to that of dwarf planets in the Kuiper Belt, like Charon and Makemake," the researchers explain.

One puzzling feature of the Cat's Tail is the size and porosity of its dust particles. They're micron-sized and very porous. "How you can get dust grains that small that are as porous as we see here, no one really understands, and that's part of why we study this feature," Avsar said.

"It's possible that the velocities involved in the collisions were so high that the carbon grains vaporized, turned into gas, and then recondensed as they cooled, and that's how they became so porous," said co-author Kevin Wagner, an assistant research professor at the U of A Steward Observatory.

https://www.youtube.com/embed/6l8QXrqBZM8
This animation shows how the Cat's Tail was created when two dwarf planets collided.

Aside from the Cat's Tail, the new Hubble observations also revealed a strange warp in Beta Pictoris' dust halo. "We also find a convex/concave morphological asymmetry in the outer halo of β Pic with HST/STIS," the authors write. This could be because of interactions with Beta Pictoris B, a giant gas planet that's about ten times as massive as Jupiter. "However, dedicated follow-up dynamical modeling is needed to confirm this," the authors conclude.

One of the reasons that planetary scientists are so intrigued by Beta Pictoris is because it holds clues to our own Solar System's past. It's like an analogue of our system, only much younger. While our system is about 4.6 billion years old, Beta Pictoris is only about 23 million years old. Early systems are far more chaotic than mature systems like ours, so looking at Beta Pictoris gives researchers a window into our own Solar System's past, when collisions were much more common.

Beta Pictoris, its disk, and its exoplanets are too fascinating to ignore. It will continue to the the subject of ongoing observations. When new, powerful telescopes like the ELT and the Giant Magellan Telescope come online in the next few years, it's certain that at some point they'll examine the β Pictoris system. Both telescopes will have the powerful capability of directly imaging exoplanets in greater detail.

These powerful telescopes may also dive deeper into the Cat's Tail, and determine why such an impact created such small, porous dust grains.

Astronomers Solve the Mystery of the Sun’s Spinning Plasma Jets

2026-10-29 11:53:48

by: Andy Tomaswick (https://www.universetoday.com/authors/andy-tomaswick)

Astronomers Solve the Mystery of the Sun’s Spinning Plasma Jets

By Andy Tomaswick - October 29, 2026 11:53 AM UTC | Solar Astronomy - 4.2K reads

The torrent of corona related discoveries keeps coming. Lately we’ve covered a variety of potential solutions to the “coronal heating problem” - why the Sun’s corona is so much hotter than its surface. Now, a paper led by a team of researchers from the University of Newcastle, Indian Institute for Astrophysics, and University of Sheffield, offers another answer - that we’ve been viewing the “spicules” that form in the lower solar atmosphere all wrong.

Spicules are spike-like geysers of plasma that shoot up into the solar atmosphere at tens of kilometers per second, eventually collapsing back down towards the surface after a few minutes. There are thought to be tens of thousands of these along the solar edge, and potentially millions of them at any given time across the entire surface of the Sun. Scientists have always treated them like miniature plasma tornados, with each individual “spike” acting as an individual.

The paper calls that into question. Instead of two dimensional spikes, it provides evidence that spicules might actually be an optical illusion created by complex three dimension “curtains” of plasma. They don’t operate as individual cylinders - they are in fact part of a rippling and folded magnetic sheet that acts like a vast atmospheric drape. From our perspective - which is essentially a two dimensional one only at the edge of the Sun, the overlapping folds create the illusion of a series of discrete, spinning jets.

https://www.youtube.com/embed/EEJKypy-1NI
Fraser talks about the highest ever resolution image taken of the Sun.

Unlike many of the other spicule-related discoveries we’ve reported on recently, this one didn’t come from new data from a new spacecraft, like Solar Orbiter or the Parker Solar Probe - it came from a simulation. The researchers used a computer simulation tool called Pencil Code to model the magnetohydrodynamics of the convection zone beneath the solar surface all the way up through the lower corona. One of the most notable features of that environment was the acoustic pounding, almost like a drum, that the churning granules of plasma beneath the Sun’s surface delivers to its atmosphere.

That pounding launches the equivalent of sound waves up into the atmosphere itself - though in this case, they are magnetohydrodynamic waves that directly follow magnetic field lines. As they travel higher, the gas surrounding them gets thinner, and they grow into huge bow shocks. When they eventually break, those shock waves blast part of the atmosphere upward, creating giant “curtains” of plasma thousands of kilometers into the Sun’s atmosphere.

Which all sounds very similar to what other research has already said about this process. This obviously wasn’t the first time someone has tried to model the connection between the Sun’s surface and atmosphere. One of the paper’s key findings was modeling what space observatories would actually see. From a top-down perspective, the plasma that was blasted up by these waves looks like a sheet arranged into accordion-like folds. But, if a telescope was looking at the edge of the Sun, the integration of those folds made it appear like a single line - a spicule. The spicules “movements” could even be understood as the curtains swaying and curling over time.

https://www.youtube.com/embed/aeXzGXKjm0Y
Video showing solar spicules, as seen by Hinode in 2007. Credit - WebMaster Hinode YouTube Channel

Luckily, there was some real data that could back up these modeled findings. Instead of one of the newer spacecraft, the researchers turned to the Interface Region Imaging Spectrograph (IRIS) and the Hinode satellite, originally launched in 2013 and 2006 respectively. Data from both had shown spicules undergoing some bizarre motions. But when the data was lined up with simulated data from the model, those bizarre motions matched well with a curving, twisting curtain. It appeared that the vertical straws of the spicules could instead be a projection of a rumpled curtain.

But perhaps the more interesting discovery was of something that sounds very like spicules, but are actually a different phenomenon altogether. The researchers used automated vortex-detection algorithms on their model and found giant columns of rotating plasma that could stretch all the way out to the corona. However, unlike spicules, these are huge whirlpools hundreds of kilometers across, rotating at up to twenty kilometers per second. The scientists named them Coronal Swirling Conduits, or CoSCos.

CoSCos appear to come in two different types. One is created when magnetic field lines snap and break in the solar atmosphere. That snapping and breaking is a primary suspect in the coronal heating mystery, but obviously has a major impact on the surrounding Sun’s environment too. A second creation path is tied to the edges of the spicule curtains - they are much denser than the “void” that makes up most of the rest of the corona, causing huge pressure and density differences along their edges. That mismatch generates something called baroclinic vorticity - on Earth it’s equivalent to how ocean breezes colliding with warm air from farther inland can stir up coastal weather systems. But in fact, as the spicule curtains themselves begin to lose energy and float back down to the surface, the massive tornadoes they kicked up will actually pull them back up into the corona.

That is the pathway by which these spicule sheets contribute to the coronal heating problem. CoSCos pull spicular sheet plasma back up from the surface into the corona, powering the solar wind and providing energy that heats the area. Or at least that’s how it works in theory. Observations of rotating bunches of spicules from instruments like Hinode and IRIS affirm where CoSCos exist, showing that rotating spicules mark regions where these theoretical giant plasma tornadoes transport energy into the solar atmosphere. But there’s still a lot we don’t know about this process - maybe we’ll get some more insight from all of the fancy new solar observation equipment that is currently looking at our beloved star.

Moon Habitats May Use Layered Mylar to Block Extreme Heat

Moon Habitats May Use Layered Mylar to Block Extreme Heat

By Laurence Tognetti, MSc - October 29, 2026 02:24 AM UTC | Space Exploration - 5.6K reads

Living on the Moon, with either trained astronauts or regular civilians, could become a regular occurrence in the near future. However, it’s easier said than done, as scientists and engineers continue developing new and advanced ways for protecting humans in the harsh lunar environment, which contrasts the mild and habitable temperatures of the Earth. The most important aspect is focusing on keeping humans alive, and this includes temperature regulation within the habitats. However, how can you maintain temperature regulation while being flexible enough to withstand physical changes like moving, handling, and compression and expansion?

Now, a team of researchers from the University of Pennsylvania might have contributed to future human missions to the Moon as they unveiled a novel design for providing insulation while delivering the flexibility for withstanding long-term use. The findings were published in ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces. The primary motivation being to fill a knowledge gap regarding how to produce and use materials capable of keeping humans alive while withstanding the long-term wear and tear of the harsh lunar environment, specifically extreme temperature changes.

https://www.youtube.com/embed/hCgllDUvjMk

To overcome this, the researchers developed layered Mylar sheets designed to withstand vacuum environments while being flexible enough for long-term use. The team evaluated Mylar thickness measured of 2.5 μm (micrometers / microns), 5 μm , and 12.5 μm along with evaluating layer density ranging between 80 to 400 layers per millimeter, with the 2.5 μm , 5 μm , and 12.5 μm stacked at 400, 200, and 80 layers, respectively. The team then subjected each layered stack to a series of rigorous tests involving vacuum conditions and a wide range of temperatures. This included subjecting the layered stacks to pressures equivalent to 200 pounds of pressure on a fingertip-sized area.

In the end, the researchers were surprised to favor the medium (5 μ"m" ) to thicker (12.5 μ"m" ) layered stacks, despite hypothesizing that the thinner (2.5 μ"m" ) layered stack. They found that the thinner layers enabled certain microparticles in the thinner sheets to enable greater heat distribution, meaning they’re not very good insulators. In contrast, the medium and thicker layers were found to have their microparticles to not be in contact as much, enabling greater levels of insulation. Additionally, the researchers found resiliency of the stacks exceeded the researchers’ expectations.

“You could use these stacks to insulate a lunar habitation, but astronauts will inevitably have little accidents where they bump into them,” said Dr. Igor Bargatin, who is Associate Professor in Mechanical Engineering and Applied Mechanics at UPenn and a co-author on the study. “When we’re talking about long-term habitation on the moon, it’s really important to have tough materials that can last long enough.”

https://www.youtube.com/embed/bWMmyQu0Zog

This study comes as NASA has ambitious plans to not only send humans back to the Moon for the first time since 1972, but with the long-term goal of establishing a permanent human presence on the lunar surface. While NASA is targeting the lunar south pole region due to the large deposits of water ice within the depths of certain craters, developing the technologies to ensure humans can both work and live on the lunar surface for long periods is essential for establishing a long-term presence.

As noted, the lunar surface experiences extreme temperature swings, ranging from 70 to 127 degrees Celsius (158 to 260 degrees Fahrenheit) in the Sun and between minus 238 to minus 247 degrees Celsius (minus 396 to minus 413 degrees Fahrenheit in the shade. While one day on the Moon is 29.5 Earth days, half of this is continuous daytime, and the other half is continuous nighttime. Therefore, developing technologies that can withstand these temperature swings and long-term exposure is paramount for humans to establish a long-term presence on the Moon.

How could layered mylar help insulate Moon habitats in the coming years and decades? Only time will tell, and this is why we science!

Virgin Galactic Flies Next-Gen Space Plane to New Home With New Name

Virgin Galactic Flies Next-Gen Space Plane to New Home With New Name

By Alan Boyle - October 28, 2026 11:22 PM UTC | Missions - 6.3K reads

Virgin Galactic is back in the spaceflight spotlight with the unveiling of a shiny new object: an updated Delta-class suborbital space plane with an exotic name.

More than two decades after SpaceShipOne won a $10 million X Prize for private spaceflight, Virgin Galactic ferried its third-generation rocket ship from its factory in Mesa, Ariz., to its home base at Spaceport America in New Mexico on Oct. 28. The spaceship was named Apeiron in an online contest that drew votes from more than 80 countries. Don't pronounce the word like "ape-iron"; it's actually "app-err-on," as in the Greek word for "Boundless."

“This morning, we gave Apeiron a name. This afternoon, Apeiron came home,” Virgin Galactic CEO Michael Colglazier said.

The new craft works on the same principles as the original SpaceShipOne: It will be launched from the belly of a twin-fuselage mothership at high altitude, blast itself upward to the edge of space, and feather its wings during descent to ease its gliding return to a runway landing.

After SpaceShipOne's prize-winning flights in 2004, its design was modified to produce two SpaceShipTwo rocket planes for Virgin Galactic. The first SpaceShipTwo, VSS Enterprise, broke up during a flight test in 2014, killing one pilot and severely injuring the other. The second SpaceShipTwo, VSS Unity, successfully flew more than 30 spacefliers — including Virgin Galactic founder Richard Branson. Unity's last commercial spaceflight came in 2024, clearing the way for Virgin Galactic's Delta-class upgrade.

The Delta-class design is aimed at beefing up the thermal protection system for Virgin Galactic's space planes, as well as streamlining the manufacturing and maintenance process. Virgin Galactic says it's aiming to launch each of its next-generation space planes twice per week, with a vehicle lifetime of more than 500 missions.

https://www.youtube.com/embed/cCEyPCvYctQ

The flight from Mesa to Spaceport America took an hour and a half, with Apeiron firmly attached to the underside of a mothership called Eve for the entire trip. Eve was piloted by Michael Masucci and Dan Alix, with flight test engineer Tori Crichton on board.

“Seeing the ship fly beneath Eve to Spaceport America was a moment years in the making," Colglazier said. "To our team and our astronaut community: Your Spaceship has arrived.”

Now that Apeiron is at its home base, Virgin Galactic's engineering team will complete software integration and validation while preparing for flight tests. The company plans to start flying customers who have paid as much as $750,000 for a suborbital trip to space by mid-2027.

A second Delta-class space plane is already taking shape at Virgin Galactic's Spaceship Factory in Mesa, and teams are preparing a rocket motor assembly line. Apeiron's younger sibling — which hasn't yet been named — is expected to begin commercial operations in the latter half of 2027.

If Virgin Galactic sticks to its schedule, it will have the suborbital spaceflight market all to itself, at least temporarily. NASA's New UV Space Telescope Will Launch in 2031

By Evan Gough - October 28, 2026 10:06 PM UTC | Telescopes - 7K reads

Most of us could spend our entire lives looking at the night sky and see very few changes. The Moon becomes more or less illuminated, the planets drift placidly through the sky, satellites and the ISS intrude for a few moments at a time, and the bright, momentary streak of a meteor punctuates the occasional evening of star-gazing.

If we're really lucky, we get a chance to witness one of the prominent comets: a regular visitor like Halley's Comet, or a once-in-several-millenia comet like Hale-Bopp. A very small number of us might be truly fortunate and witness a distant supernova.

But the apparent uneventfulness of the night sky is an illusion, even for those with a typical backyard telescope. It's the result of a selection bias based on our ability to see only visible light. The reality of the night sky is much different. Calamitous and transient events are nearly continuous, if you have the right eyes to see. Many astronomical phenomena, like stellar flares and exploding stars, only reveal themselves fully in ultraviolet (UV) light, which is beyond our ability to see without technological help.

Soon we'll have a new space telescope that sees exclusively in UV. NASA has announced that UVEX, the UltraViolet EXplorer, will launch in 2031. The UVEX mission, which will be led by CalTech, will perform an all-sky survey in near and far-ultraviolet.

"Every time you look at the skies in a new wavelength, you learn something new and unexpected." Kareem El-Badry, UVEX team member, Caltech.

"UVEX is a bold leap forward in our exploration of the ultraviolet universe," said Caltech President and professor of astronomy Ray Jayawardhana.

Light across all wavelengths, from radio to gamma-rays, floods our Universe. We can only really understand what's creating all of the light if we can see all of the light. UVEX will fill a gap in our observations by providing an all-sky survey in UV that will complement surveys by other telescopes like the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope, the Vera Rubin Observatory, and the ESA's Euclid Space Telescope.

When most people think of telescopes and space telescopes, they likely imagine powerful telescopes that can see long distances, bringing exquisite views of distant objects into view. After all, images of nebulae and other objects are eye candy, they grace websites landing pages and help generate public interest in space and astronomy.

But survey telescopes play a huge role in science. The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope will perform three separate surveys in infrared, the Vera Rubin Observatory will image the night sky repeatedly over a decade, and Euclid is performing three deep-field surveys in visible light, capturing over 10 billion sources (galaxies). Another NASA telescope, SPHEREx, is also conducting an all-sky survey in near-infrared.

These vast datasets are invaluable in modern astronomy, and UVEX will contribute to this growing, detailed picture of the Universe. It will also gather focused observations on individual targets.

This figure shows how UVEX will provide deep, two-band UV data to complement planned deep, wide-field (> 10,000 deg2) optical and near-IR surveys by Rubin and Euclid. Together, they will build a multiwavelength survey of the sky. Image Credit: Kulkarni et al. 2023.

"In its two-year baseline mission, UVEX will perform a multi-cadence synoptic all-sky survey 50/100 times deeper than GALEX in the NUV/FUV, cadenced surveys of the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds, rapid target of opportunity followup, as well as spectroscopic followup of samples of stars and galaxies," the authors of a white paper about UVEX wrote. GALEX was a NASA ultraviolet space telescope whose mission ended in 2013. But UVEX will be up to 100 times more sensitive than GALEX.

In particular, UVEX will observe nearby low-mass galaxies that are bright in UV, and those observations will complement observations by the JWST and the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope (NGRST).

This infographic explains some of what's behind the UVEX mission. UVEX will focus on relatively nearby low-mass galaxies and their bright ultraviolet light. These same galaxies exist billions of light years away, but their light is red-shifted, meaning its now infrared light. The JWST and the Roman Space Telescope can see them. By examining the closer counterparts to these galaxies in UV, UVEX will complement the infrared observations from the other two telescopes. Image Credit: Caltech/Robert Hurt (IPAC-SElab)

The low-mass galaxies that UVEX will focus on are important in the Universe for the role they play. In a paper describing the UVEX mission, the authors explained that UVEX is based on three "pillars," the first of which is low-mass galaxies.

"First, UVEX will explore the low-mass, low-metallicity galaxy frontier through imaging and spectroscopic surveys that will probe key aspects of the evolution of galaxies by understanding how star formation and stellar evolution at low metallicities affect the growth and evolution of low-metallicity, low-mass galaxies in the local universe," the authors wrote. This galaxy population contains half of the Universe's mass, and they're analogs for the Universe's first galaxies.

"Second, UVEX will explore the dynamic universe through time-domain surveys, and prompt spectroscopic followup capability will probe the environments, energetics, and emission processes in the early aftermaths of gravitational wave-discovered compact object mergers, discover hot, fast UV transients, and diagnose the early stages of stellar explosions," the authors explain.

Lastly, UVEX, like other survey missions, will generate a vast treasure trove of UV data accessible to researchers studying a wide range of objects and phenomena. This is very similar to what the ESA's recently-completed Gaia mission did. Gaia's data is used in a wide variety of research areas, and will likely be relied upon for a long time.

This infographic puts UVEX into context with other telescopes and surveys. It also shows how it stacks up against other UV observatories. Image Credit: Kulkarni et al. 2023.

UVEX will bring things into view that were simply too dim to be seen before. Explosions, stellar mergers, tidal disruptions events, and more will now will be visible more than ever. These transient events are some of the most dramatic happenings in the Universe, and along with with its steady surveying activity, UVEX will be able to quickly point at transient events and watch as they unfold.

While several powerful telescopes have launched in the last few years, this is the first new UV telescope in 20 years. And it will be the very first UV mission to create deep-sky maps in UV. With its extreme sensitivity and spectroscope, it will discern the different wavelengths of light coming from distant objects, clarifying which chemical elements are present.

UVEX will also complement gravitational-wave observatories. Gravitational waves are ripples in space-time (predicted by Einstein) that are generated when extremely dense objects, like neutron stars and black holes, merge. When generational waves are detected, alerts go out to other telescopes which then observe them in wavelengths including radio and gamma rays. When UVEX is launched, it will add UV observations.

"With UVEX, we will now be able to follow up on gravitational-wave events in ultraviolet light," explains UVEX team member Mansi Kasliwal (PhD '11), a professor of astronomy at Caltech and director of the Palomar Observatory. UVEX's detailed spectra will reveal more information than ever about these events.

Each new mission and telescope is launched with a firm idea of its goals and expected results. But as we keep seeing with telescopes, including the powerful JWST and its observations of early supermassive galaxies, each new telescope also generates some suprises.

"Every time you look at the skies in a new wavelength," said Kareem El-Badry, associate professor of astronomy at Caltech and a member of the UVEX team, "you learn something new and unexpected."
