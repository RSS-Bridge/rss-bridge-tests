Toplines

Iran is unlikely to respond positively to a recent US letter proposing comprehensive direct negotiations. A senior US official and an Iranian official told the New York Times on October 28 that US Vice President JD Vance sent Supreme National Security Council (SNSC) Secretary Major General Mohsen Rezaei a letter through Qatari mediators that proposed comprehensive direct negotiations.[1] An unspecified US official added that US officials reportedly addressed the letter to Rezaei because the United States assessed that he could influence Islamic Revolutionary Guards Corps (IRGC) officials and hardliners who oppose an agreement with the United States.[2] It is unclear how the United States reached this assessment. Rezaei recently reiterated hardline negotiating positions, which were demands that the United States end the war on all fronts, release frozen assets, and lift the naval blockade before negotiations over anything else resume.[3] The SNSC is Iran’s highest national security and foreign policy decision-making body.[4] Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei appointed Rezaei — his former senior military adviser and a former IRGC commander — as SNSC secretary on August 9, which may have strengthened Mojtaba’s control over the SNSC and reinforced the regime’s hardline orientation.[5] The officials also told the New York Times that Vance stated in the letter to Rezaei that negotiations must address Iran’s nuclear program from the outset, which is antithetical to Rezaei’s stated positions on negotiations. Rezaei’s views are consistent with the Iranian official view, which demands that the United States first implement Iran’s conditions, including lifting the US naval blockade, releasing Iranian frozen assets, and recognizing Iran’s claimed authority over the Strait of Hormuz.[6] IRGC-affiliated Tasnim News Agency reported on October 29 that Iran had not yet responded to the letter and still considers any departure from its seven conditions “unacceptable.”[7]

The Yemeni government accused the Houthis of using chemical weapons in combat against Yemeni government-aligned forces in central Taiz Governorate on October 28.[8] The Yemeni government provided no evidence to substantiate these claims, and no local sources provided any proof of the reported chemical attacks. The seriousness of these claims nonetheless requires reporting, and ISW-CTP will continue to monitor the Yemeni news and social media space for indications that chemical attacks occurred. The Yemeni government’s Media Office in Taiz Governorate cited combat reports that the Houthis fired artillery shells that may have contained “internationally banned toxic substances” while engaging Yemeni government-aligned forces near Kahlan, Sabr al Mawadim District, and al Aqroud, al Misrakh District, both in central Taiz Governorate.[9] The Houthis have repeatedly failed to consolidate control over these combat areas in recent days amid Yemeni government-aligned forces’ efforts to recapture nearby territory and break the Houthis’ encirclement of central Taiz Governorate.[10] The media office also claimed that local civilians, including pro-government local resistance forces, experienced respiratory complications and loss of consciousness in “previous incidents” but did not provide further detail.[11] Respiratory effects from a chemical attack would suggest that the Houthis used tear gas, chlorine gas, or a similar typically non-lethal chemical compound, but that the chemical was present in sufficient concentration to cause unconsciousness.[12]

Yemeni government officials, including Information Minister Muammar Eryani, have previously accused the Houthis of procuring materials for a chemical warfare program and manufacturing chemical weapons.[13] ISW-CTP cannot verify the veracity of these claims, however. ISW-CTP has also not observed any reports of Houthi chemical weapons use in other major combat areas, such as Taiz City or the Red Sea coastal plain.

Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al Zaydi is continuing his attempts to balance between the United States and Iranian-backed Iraqi actors. Unspecified Iranian-backed Iraqi militias have reached an “understanding” with Zaydi to disarm in exchange for “key” government positions, according to sources speaking to Iraqi media on October 29.[14] The sources said that Zaydi would soon appoint a militia-linked figure as the secretary-general of the Council of Ministers, a position that a figure aligned with Shia nationalist cleric Muqtada al Sadr has held for more than two administrations.[15] ISW-CTP similarly assessed that the federal government agreed to grant three Iraqi militias control over some government ministries on October 15 to both incentivize the militias that have agreed to disarm to fully comply while motivating militias that have refused to disarm to agree to the government’s still unreleased disarmament plan.[16] The Shia Coordination Framework approved some details of this plan, which aims to disarm the militias by June 2027, in late September.[17] It is unclear if the militias that reached an “understanding” with Zaydi will actually fully comply with disarmament, given the amount of room the framework’s agreement leaves for militia exploitation, per the plan’s publicly available details. (ISW-CTP has outlined militia attempts to avoid disarmament in an October 28 special report: Iranian-backed Iraqi Militias Undermine Disarmament Efforts). The United States has continuously rejected militia-affiliated individuals serving in high-level positions within the Iraqi federal government.[18]

Zaydi’s efforts to balance competing pressures also support his aims of increasing his personal power as prime minister. An unspecified framework leader told Iraqi media on October 28 that Zaydi is making changes to his administration independent of the framework.[19] Zaydi replaced the director of his office, who was a member of the former prime minister’s party, with a technocrat who had been on an Iraqi government committee that designated the Houthis and Hezbollah as terrorist organizations in 2025 and froze their assets.[20] The federal government quickly backtracked on the decision to designate the Houthis and Hezbollah, reportedly due to pressure from Iranian-backed actors.[21] Zaydi’s appointment of an individual affiliated with an Iraqi federal government decision that broadly supports US efforts to counter Iran’s Axis of Resistance could be an attempt by Zaydi to counter these militia appointments. This appointment could also be in line with Zaydi’s attempt to replace government officials affiliated with his predecessor. The framework leader said that Zaydi is to fire 69 advisors associated with Sudani.[22] The framework leader also criticized Zaydi’s independent negotiations with the militias for undermining the framework’s own negotiations.[23] Zaydi has reportedly called the framework negotiating with the militias “biased,” in that the framework includes militia members and other Iranian-backed actors.[24] Zaydi pursuing his own negotiations reflects his desire to make decisions independent of the framework but could also support US aims for the development of a sovereign and independent Iraq.

Arabian Peninsula

Saudi and Yemeni government-aligned forces are likely attempting to disrupt Houthi maritime sustainment and resupply efforts on the Red Sea coastal plain by targeting Houthi-controlled ports and vessels. Houthi and Yemeni government-aligned media reported on October 28 and 29 that Saudi or Yemeni government-aligned forces conducted several rounds of airstrikes targeting Houthi positions in Mokha and Dhubab — both on the Red Sea coast.[25] Houthi media reported that at least some of the strikes targeted the port of Mokha on October 28.[26] Yemeni government-aligned media previously reported on October 28 that strikes by Saudi or Yemeni government-aligned forces destroyed two Houthi vessels in the southern Red Sea that were likely transporting weapons from the primary Houthi port in Hudaydah to other Houthi positions near Mokha, Dhubab, or along the Red Sea Coast.[27] These reported strikes on Houthi-controlled ports and vessels are consistent with ISW-CTP’s assessment that Saudi Arabia and the Yemeni government intend to weaken Houthi forces that are engaged in frontline combat by disrupting the group’s sustainment and logistics efforts.[28] Saudi and Yemeni government-aligned forces have struck various logistical targets, including Houthi weapons depots, food storehouses, and reinforcement convoys, in recent weeks in pursuit of this objective.[29]

Reported Saudi or Yemeni government airstrikes on Houthi vessels carrying weapons transiting the southern Red Sea indicate that these forces are still capable of threatening Houthi smuggling and maritime capabilities in the Red Sea to a limited extent following their substantial territorial losses in September 2026.[30] ISW-CTP previously assessed during the Houthi Red Sea coastal plain offensive on September 10 that the Houthis’ capture of Mokha and all remaining Red Sea ports would inhibit the Yemeni government’s ability to interdict Houthi smuggling vessels.[31] Yemeni government-aligned forces have recaptured some territory on the southern Red Sea coastal plain since September 10, including areas near the Bab al Mandeb Strait, but do not control a port from which to dispatch naval counter-smuggling and reconnaissance patrols in the Red Sea.[32]

These reports also suggest that the Houthis are using sea lines of communication (SLOCs) in the southern Red Sea to resupply and equip front-line forces on the coastal plain and near the Bab al Mandeb. The Houthis are likely transporting munitions along a SLOC from Hudaydah to Mokha or Dhubab because major Houthi ground lines of communication (GLOCs) between Hudaydah and Dhubab are highly exposed to Saudi and Yemeni government airstrikes. Saudi and Yemeni government-aligned forces have conducted battlefield air interdiction along the coast since the offensive began.[33]

    • Russian Offensive Campaign Assessment, October 29, 2026

    Toplines

    Russian forces conducted devastating daytime long-range drone and loitering munition strikes against Ukraine on October 29, killing at least 19 and injuring at least 82. Ukrainian officials reported that a Russian jet-powered drone struck a commuter train at the Dnipro City railway station, Dnipropetrovsk Oblast, killing at least 14 and injuring at least 14.[1] The Ukrainian Air Force reported that Russian forces launched four jet-powered drones against Dnipro City, and that Ukrainian forces intercepted three drones.[2] Ukrainian officials reported that Russian forces also conducted a Banderol loitering munition strike against a residential area in Chernihiv City, Chernihiv Oblast, killing at least two children and injuring at least 31 people, and a drone strike against an apartment building in Kremenchuk, Poltava Oblast, killing at least three and injuring at least 32, including six children.[3] The Ukrainian Air Force also reported that Russian forces launched 112 Shahed-type strike drones (40 of which were jet-powered) and Gerbera-type strike drones and Parodiya-type decoy drones against Ukraine overnight.[4]

    The October 29 strikes are part of Russia’s continued effort to launch daytime strikes to make daily life in Ukraine untenable, inflict significant civilian casualties, and damage Ukraine’s economy. Russian forces have increased the brutality and lethality of their strike campaign throughout 2026, including conducting prolonged daytime and nighttime strike packages over several days designed to maximize civilian harm, leveraging vulnerabilities in Ukrainian air defenses, and introducing new technological adaptations like quasi-cruise missiles such as Banderols.[5] Russia’s October 29 long-range strikes, including against the Dnipro City railway station, demonstrate that Russia can inflict severe civilian harm when even a small percentage of drones penetrate Ukrainian defenses. Moscow is attempting to impose additional costs on the Ukrainian population ahead of Winter 2026-2027, as the Kremlin likely assesses that these long-range strikes can distract from Ukraine’s recent battlefield successes and ultimately force Ukraine into capitulation by making daily civilian life untenable.[6]

    Russian President Vladimir Putin attempted to falsely portray Ukraine as the party responsible for the humanitarian catastrophe in Russian-occupied Oleshky, Kherson Oblast, where the limited ceasefire continues without reported violations. Ukrainian Human Rights Commissioner Dmytro Lubinets and Russian Human Rights Commissioner Yana Lantratova reported on October 29 that there have been no ceasefire violations in Russian-occupied Oleshky for 54 hours since the beginning of the ceasefire on the morning of October 27.[7] Putin attempted on October 29 to falsely blame Ukraine for mining approaches to occupied Oleshky and creating a “humanitarian catastrophe.”[8] Russian occupation authorities, on the contrary, are fully responsible for the humanitarian crisis in occupied Oleshky, and Russian forces have been repeatedly and deliberately disrupting humanitarian aid deliveries and evacuation routes into the settlement.[9] Russian occupation officials also refused to abide by their obligations under international law to provide civilians in occupied areas with food and aid during conflict and have suspended food and medicine deliveries since May 2026.[10] The Kremlin’s repeated rejections of three Ukraine-proposed local humanitarian ceasefires forced Ukrainian forces to conduct a drone food delivery to Oleshky residents in September 2026, and Russian forces intentionally shot down the drones carrying these provisions.[11]

    The Kremlin is attempting to drive a wedge between European countries in an attempt to undermine Europe’s negotiation position on the war in Ukraine and exclude European representatives from peace talks so long as they refuse to accept Russia’s demands, including NATO’s rollback to its 1997 borders. Russian Presidential Aide Yuriy Ushakov claimed on October 29 that Europe does not have a unified and consistent position regarding Russian President Vladimir Putin’s October 27 call with Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Store and noted that Russia did not expect this “inconsistency.”[12] Ushakov also claimed that the current agreement involves only Ukraine-Russia-US trilateral talks, implying that European representatives will not be present at peace negotiations.[13] Ushakov added, however, that Russia will have to negotiate with people whom Russia “does not really like,” when commenting on the possibility of future dialogue with Europe.[14] Kremlin Spokesperson Dmitry Peskov similarly claimed on October 28 that Russia must support European representatives who understand the need for a dialogue with the Kremlin in the context of peace negotiations.[15] Russian Ministry of Foreign Affairs (MFA) Spokesperson Maria Zakharova claimed on October 28 that European leaders continue demonstrating their unwillingness to discuss “Russian interests” and are voicing “unrealistic demands on Moscow.”[16] The October 28 and 29 Russian officials’ statements indicate that the Kremlin is attempting to deny Europeans a seat at the negotiating table until they accede to Russia’s December 2021 demand that NATO curtail its presence in Europe to its 1997 borders, effectively amounting to the destruction of the NATO alliance and requiring an overhaul of Europe’s security architecture.[17] The Kremlin is also attempting to exploit Putin’s October 27 call with Stubb and Store to sow distrust among European states and thus weaken Europe’s negotiating position on the war in Ukraine.

    The Kremlin is also likely setting conditions that would require Ukraine to make unilateral concessions before Russia agrees to engage in any meaningful peace negotiations in any format. Ushakov claimed on October 29 that trilateral negotiations between the United States, Ukraine, and Russia in Abu Dhabi will happen after November 4 (Russian National Unity Day) but noted that the date will depend on the “situation on the battlefield and the overall situation.”[18] Ushakov also noted that Russia will monitor whether Ukraine conducts large-scale strikes around Russia’s November 4 celebration, implying that Russia’s negotiating position will depend on Ukraine’s halting of long-range strikes despite the fact that the Kremlin has repeatedly refused to accept mutual long-range ceasefire agreements.[19] Kremlin officials have repeatedly weaponized exaggerated claims of Russian battlefield advances as leverage during peace negotiations to extract unilateral concessions from Ukraine, often framing such concessions as prerequisites for any meaningful peace negotiations.[20] It remains unclear exactly what conditionality Ushakov’s October 29 statements refer to, but the Kremlin is likely setting conditions to establish arbitrary prerequisite demands that require unilateral Ukrainian concessions as conditions for renewing peace talks and frame Ukraine as the obstacle to peace if Ukraine fails to accept those demands.

    Toplines

    Both Russia and Ukraine are observing the limited humanitarian ceasefire intended to provide aid to the Russian-occupied Oleshky area as of October 29, but Russia may use the ceasefire as a cover to facilitate the forced transfer or deportation of Oleshky evacuees further into Russian-controlled territory or to Russia itself. The White House initially reported on October 23 that First Lady Melania Trump brokered a ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine for the occupied Oleshky area, intended to facilitate humanitarian aid deliveries and evacuations amidst a dire humanitarian catastrophe.[1] Both Russia and Ukraine confirmed the ceasefire via their human rights commissioners and Russian Chief of the General Staff Army General Valery Gerasimov.[2] The ceasefire went into effect at 0300 Kyiv time on October 27 and will last until 2259 Kyiv time on November 1.[3] Unspecified sources told the BBC on October 27 that the ceasefire has extended to all of occupied Kherson Oblast.[4] Ukrainian Ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets noted that the ceasefire will occur in three stages: a total cessation of combat activity; humanitarian aid delivery; and the free movement of civilians out of the town.[5] The evacuation portion of the ceasefire will begin on the morning of October 29. Both Russia and Ukraine are adhering to ceasefire terms as of October 29.[6]

    External reporting on Russia’s conduct of evacuations from the Oleshky area is raising concerns. Evacuations will not be conducted across the Dnipro River directly into Ukrainian-held territory, likely due to the dangers posed by transit across heavily mined water and marsh areas.[7] This means that evacuations will only take place via Russian-occupied Ukraine and Russia itself. The Financial Times (FT), citing people familiar with the matter, reported on October 27 that Russian officials facilitating evacuations will transfer civilians who chose to leave Oleshky to occupied Crimea before they can return to Ukraine.[8] Fox News published reported ceasefire documents on October 28 that state that Oleshky residents who wish to return to Ukraine will be transferred first to Anapa, Krasnodar Krai, and then to Gomel, Belarus.[9] The transfer of evacuees through several Russian-controlled destinations within occupied Ukraine, Russia, and Belarus (which is effectively a Russian vassal state) provides Russian authorities with ample opportunities to exert coercive influence over these evacuees, potentially to convince them not to return to Ukraine. Kherson City council deputy Oksana Pohomii told The Telegraph on October 27 that residents of the Oleshky area are “deeply concerned” that Russian authorities will deport them to Russia as opposed to evacuating them to Ukraine.[10] ISW has observed substantial evidence of Ukrainian frontline evacuees receiving Russian documents and facing other forms of coercive pressure as part of Russian-led humanitarian evacuation efforts — a template that may be applied to Oleshky evacuations as well.[11] Russia may use humanitarian evacuations from the Oleshky area as a means of legitimizing the forced transfer or deportation of evacuees further into Russian-occupied Ukraine or to Russia itself.

    Deportation and Forcible Transfer of Ukrainian Citizens, Including Children

    Russian officials have deported a 10-year-old girl from Donetsk Oblast to Russia and are distorting the child’s story to frame the deportation as humanitarianism in Russian mass media. Kremlin Commissioner on Children’s Rights Maria Lvova-Belova claimed on October 21 that her office facilitated the transfer of a 10-year-old girl to the custody of her uncle in Russia.[12] Lvova-Belova claimed that Russian soldiers found the child alone in a basement, brought her food, and protected her until they were able to safely evacuate her.[13] Russian officials and state media, however, have repeatedly distorted the 10-year-old girl’s true circumstances. Bring Kids Back activist Oleksandra Dvoretska reported that the girl had never met or seen the uncle prior to their alleged reunification.[14] Russian investigative outlet Agentsvo used facial recognition technology to identify that the child had actually been in a Russian-controlled children’s home in occupied Shakhtarsk, around 75 kilometers from Kostyantynivka, since at least June 5, suggesting that Lvova-Belova used the child for propagandistic purposes to stage her “evacuation” from Kostyantynivka. ISW has also previously reported on how Russian authorities had purposefully suppressed information about the child and have not looked for her relatives.[15]

    These discrepancies suggest that the Russian officials are weaponizing the child’s story to construct a false narrative of Russian humanitarianism in Ukraine. Russian state media outlets, including TASS and Pervyi Kanal, have widely circulated this story to portray Russian forces as protecting Ukrainian children against Ukrainian battlefield cruelty. Lvova-Belova published a video on October 21 of the girl’s alleged reunification with her “uncle,” in which the girl wears a cap with Russian camouflage and the inscription “Spets,” referring to Russian special operations forces “Spetsnaz.”[16] The 10-year-old girl’s deportation, and its related coverage in Russian media, has demonstrated how Russian officials weaponize Ukrainian children for a wide variety of propaganda purposes.

    Russia’s child welfare and humanitarian assistance policies in occupied Kherson Oblast are leading to family-child separation and the transfer of children further to Russian-state run institutions further within occupied territory. Nova Kakhovka occupation head Vladimir Oganesov claimed on October 14 that the Kherson Oblast occupation Department of Guardianship, Trusteeship, and Patronage removed four children from their families due to unspecified threats to the children’s lives or health.[17] The Department will place one child in the Henichesk Central Hospital in occupied Henichesk and three children at the Novokyivka Social Rehabilitation Center for Minors. Both facilities are registered as Russian state budgetary institutions after the occupation of Kherson Oblast in 2022, meaning that the Russian state has ownership of all their property and assets and directly funds them.[18] This incident may raise concerns under international law due to the fact that occupation authorities asserted their ability to remove children from their families, without providing a sound legal basis for their removal, and place them into Russian-run state institutions.

    Sociocultural Control

    Russia is intensifying efforts to standardize Russian language and literature education in occupied Ukraine as a tool for integrating Ukrainian youth into the Russian sociocultural sphere but may be encountering some challenges to the practical implementation of its language policies in occupied areas. Russian Presidential Advisor Elena Yampolskaya told Donetsk News Agency on October 15 that schoolchildren in Russia and Russian-occupied territories of Ukraine will begin using standardized Russian language and literature textbooks in September 2027 or September 2028.[19] Yampolskaya further claimed that Russian occupation authorities will mandate updated textbooks on the Russian language for 10th graders and on literature for 5th, 6th, 10th, and 11th graders in occupied Ukraine starting from September 1, 2027.[20]This development follows the 10th Congress of Russian Language Specialists at Sevastopol University on October 6–10, during which over 200 Russian language teachers, lecturers, and philologists discussed the importance of the Russian language as a critical factor in societal consolidation and the integration of occupied territories.[21]

    Russian efforts to impose strict Russian language education in occupied Ukraine appear to be facing challenges in practical implementation. Occupied Crimean-based Russian language teacher Viktoria Grinchenko told RIA Novosti on October 19 that some schoolchildren in occupied Ukraine will not be able to take the Unified State Exam (Russia’s standardized university entrance exam) for at least 10 more years due to their lack of Russian language writing abilities.[22] This reported gap in actual Russian language ability indicates that existing Russian attempts to standardize language instruction in occupied territories of Ukraine have not achieved their intended goals. The planned distribution of standardized textbooks may demonstrate the Kremlin’s renewed push to Russify occupied Ukrainian territories that individual teachers and administrators in occupied territories have failed to implement fully.

    Militarization of Occupied Areas

    The Crimean occupation administration continues to encourage youth participation in drone competitions to interest and prepare Ukrainian youth for future service in the Russian military. Sevastopol occupation governor Mikhail Razvozhaev stated on October 15 that 100 Sevastopol children participated in the online first round of the All-Russia Drone Piloting Championship, “Pilots of the Future,” with 50 children moving on to the next round in Moscow from November 17 to 22.[23] Occupation administrators across occupied Ukraine, and particularly in Crimea, have intensified efforts to interest youth in operating military technology through extracurricular activities such as drone racing competitions[24] Crimean occupation authorities have explicitly stated that the goal of these drone-related extracurricular activities is to establish a long-term training pipeline that develops the technical skills of talented children and youth and later integrates them into the personnel reserve for drone-related military professions in the Russian military.[25] These efforts support Russia’s long-term objectives of militarizing children in occupied Ukraine and using the population of occupied territories to form a reserve supply of future Russian military personnel.

    Russian occupation officials continue to initiate youth in occupied territories into Russian state institutions through military-patriotic programming. Occupied Donetsk Oblast-based outlet Donetsk News Agency reported on October 14 that the Karchun youth Cossack squad held an induction ceremony for new members on the Feast of the Protection of the Holy Virgin as part of the federal “Youth and Children” project.[26] Each inductee expressed their acceptance of Orthodox faith and service to the “Fatherland” as a part of the ceremony. Mariupol occupation head Anton Koltsov claimed on October 17 that an oath-taking ceremony for young cadets also took place at one of five schools in occupied Mariupol offering the program, marking a “significant milestone” for cadets who will undergo intensive military training alongside their standard school curriculum.[27] Both cadet classes and Cossack squads are direct means of Russifying Ukrainian youth by normalizing their interaction with two key interlinked Kremlin-controlled institutions: the Russian Orthodox Church (ROC) and the Russian military. Many Cossack organizations and leaders are closely intertwined with the ROC, and Cossack programming exposes Ukrainian youth to religious narratives that legitimize Russian aggression against Ukraine as a sacred duty.[28] Cadet classes also normalize participation in Russian military activities among Ukrainian youth through the education system. Youth programs aimed at integrating Ukrainian children into Russian state institutions are therefore part of a larger Kremlin effort to instill pro-Russian values and use the education apparatus in occupied territories to normalize and encourage future service in the Russian army.

    Administrative and Bureaucratic Control

    The Mariupol occupation administration appears to have begun a new wave of forced evictions using “squatting” (unauthorized use of land) laws. Mariupol-based occupation outlet Mariupol 24 published a video segment on October 16 interviewing residents of an apartment complex on Mariupol’s central Myru avenue (Prospekt Myru).[29] One woman interviewed in the video stated that every apartment but two in the building received eviction notices on the grounds that they were occupying the apartments “illegally,” despite the majority of residents having legal ownership over their apartments. Another woman notes that the majority of the building’s residents are in the “compensation queue” for new housing following the destruction of their properties during the Russian siege on Mariupol in 2022, but that occupation authorities have done nothing to provide them with promised accommodations for at least six months. Mariupol deputy occupation head Ignat Yeremchuk spoke to building residents in the footage, asserting that he has observed evidence of people squatting but noting that some unspecified categories of residents will be able to maintain their homes.

    The use of “squatting” provisions to evict residents from their homes is consistent with a broader pattern of the Mariupol occupation authority using various administrative and regulatory loopholes to expel individuals from their legally held properties.[30] In the instance of the Myru Avenue apartment building, it is likely that occupation officials are using the presence of Ukrainian property documents (eg. documents formalized before the invasion, or those still written in the Ukrainian language) as grounds to claim that the resident is living in their own apartment illegally. Myru Avenue is in central Mariupol, and residences along the street are generally considered more desirable. The occupation administration is likely evicting Ukrainian residents from buildings in this area in order to offer renovated apartments in central Mariupol to Russian citizens from Russia, or those otherwise loyal to the occupation government.[31]

    Russian occupation officials may be using civilian evacuations from frontline areas to pressure Ukrainian civilians into accepting Russian passports. Donetsk People’s Republic (DNR) occupation Deputy Prime Minister Ilya Yemelyanov claimed on October 26 that the majority of the 1,200 Ukrainian civilians whom Russian forces evacuated from Kostyantynivka have already received Russian passports.[32] Russian occupation officials claimed that evacuees were taken from Kostyantynivka to a “temporary accommodation center” (TAC) presumably deeper within Russian occupied territory, where they received humanitarian assistance and immediate support in applying for Russian citizenship.[33] Yemelyanov noted that these evacuees can choose to either apply for Russian citizenship at their places of residence (presumably in Kostyantynivka) or in “other regions of Russia”. ISW has observed an ongoing trend of the passportization of evacuees from frontline settlements at TACs, suggesting that even if evacuation is necessary for humanitarian reasons, Russian authorities quickly use the circumstances of the evacuation to forcibly issue Russian citizenship to Ukrainians.[34]

    International law permits an occupying power to evacuate civilian populations from a given area for humanitarian purposes as long as that evacuation is temporary.[35] The process occurring in Russian TACs, however, suggests that Russian officials are using the evacuation process to permanently grant Ukrainians Russian citizenship, and potentially to facilitate the relocation of Ukrainians to areas further in occupied territories or within the Russian Federation.[36] These frontline evacuations may therefore constitute a violation of international law, as evacuations catalyze a prolonged and potentially permanent process of forcing an increasing number of Ukrainians into assuming Russian national identity, rather than returning to Ukrainian-controlled territories.

    Physical and Legal Repressions

    Russian occupation authorities continue to prosecute Ukrainian minors for alleged terrorism-related cases. Occupation authorities and investigators in Luhansk Oblast claimed on October 13 that they referred six minors to court for allegedly conducting 20 arson attacks against electrical substations, gas infrastructure facilities, cellular towers, and vehicles in occupied Sverdlovsk Raion between May and September 2025.[37] An investigation allegedly found that a Ukrainian special services representative recruited the minors through Telegram to conduct the attacks in exchange for money, causing over 2.5 million rubles in damages. Five out of six of the suspected minors are currently awaiting sentencing in pre-trial detention, while one of the accused remains wanted by law enforcement. These arrests are part of a broader Russian campaign of arresting minors in occupied territories, which intensified after Russian President Vladimir Putin signed a law in November 2025 that lowered the age of criminal responsibility for terrorism-related charges from 16 to 14 years old.[38]

    The Russian Southern District Military Court previously sentenced three Ukrainian teenagers to extensive prison sentences on likely fabricated terrorism charges in March 2026.[39] Ukrainian officials reported that the three minors have spent around three years in various Russian detention centers, including in facilities where Russia is known to torture and kill Ukrainian civilians.[40] Minors from occupied territories accused of terrorism-related charges have generally faced harsh sentences of 10 to 20 years of imprisonment, potentially keeping them in prison until their late 30s. Independent human rights research organization Parubets Analytics published an investigation finding that Russian courts have sentenced at least 158 teenagers in terrorism and sabotage cases since 2022.[41] ISW has previously reported on Russia’s use of occupation-controlled courts as a coercive tool of suppressing pro-Ukrainian attitudes and maintaining social control in occupied territories.[42] The recent charges against minors demonstrated the continued expansion of Russia’s repressive judicial apparatus in occupied Ukraine to target younger generations of Ukrainians.

    Courts in Russia and occupied Ukraine continue to harshly punish residents of occupied Ukraine in high-profile treason or terrorism cases. ISW collected information about seven cases heard in the Kherson Oblast occupation Court, the Zaporizhia Oblast occupation Court, the Krasnodar Regional Court, and the Southern District Military Court in Rostov-on-Don concerning residents of occupied Ukraine between October 19 and 26.[43] Two of the cases involved allegations that the defendants monitored locations of Russian military equipment and personnel and shared the information with Ukrainian intelligence.[44] The defendants in these cases received roughly 12 years in prison each. The other five cases concerned situations where the defendants had allegedly planned an act of direct sabotage, such as planting explosive devices near occupation infrastructure or planning attacks against occupation law enforcement.[45] The defendants in these cases received between eight and 24 years in prison.

    Certain conditions and informational discrepancies surrounding some of the trials call the legitimacy of the charges into question. One such case concerns Vladimir Ananyev, a 77-year-old Crimean activist who was sentenced to 18 years in prison on October 22 for allegedly planning an attack on Crimea occupation head Sergei Aksyonov.[46] Ananyev has repeatedly pleaded not guilty to the charges, and information from independent media and legal professionals indicates that the claims against Ananyev are likely falsified.     The Institute for the Study of War (ISW) and The Critical Threats Project (CTP) at the American Enterprise Institute are publishing updates Monday through Friday to provide analysis on the war with Iran and the broader Middle East. Weekday updates cover events from the past 24-hour period, whereas weekend updates are abbreviated and cover the Houthi-Saudi conflict and major breaking developments related to the war with Iran. 

    Key Takeaways

    1. Russia is actively supporting Houthi operations to disrupt global energy markets by providing the Houthis with satellite imagery of Saudi oil and military infrastructure. Russian satellites have routinely been positioned above major Saudi energy and military sites in the days prior to and after Houthi attacks on those sites since July 2026.

    Toplines

    Russia is actively supporting Houthi operations to disrupt global energy markets by providing the Houthis with satellite imagery of Saudi oil and military infrastructure. A Switzerland-based geospatial intelligence firm told the Associated Press on October 28 that it has consistently observed Russian military satellites positioned above major Saudi energy and military sites in the days prior to and following Houthi drone and missile strikes on Saudi Arabia.[1] Russian satellite activity followed a similar pattern around attacks on Saudi infrastructure originating from Iraq, where the Houthis maintain a presence and have launched attacks on Saudi Arabia with Iraqi militias. A Saudi official and a high-ranking Houthi official separately told the Associated Press that Russia is providing satellite-based targeting intelligence to the Houthis, reportedly through Iranian military advisers who are operating in Yemen.[2] Russian pre- and post-strike imagery very likely enables the Houthis and Iranian military advisers to conduct battle damage assessments of their strikes.[3] The geospatial intelligence firm observed 40 instances of Russian satellite positioning over Saudi military and energy sites between July 1 and October 19, including the September 10 drone strike on the Saudi East-West Pipeline.[4] Russian support is enabling the Iranian-supported Houthi campaign to constrain Saudi Arabia’s ability to export oil during a global energy crisis that Iran has caused by attacking ships in the Strait of Hormuz.[5]

    Russian support for Houthi efforts to disrupt Saudi energy exports is consistent with long-standing Russian support for Iran and its regional partners, particularly the Houthis. Russia previously provided the Houthis with satellite-based targeting intelligence to help the group attack US vessels and international shipping in the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden in 2023 and 2024, for example.[6] The Houthis supported Russia in turn by promising to not target Russian vessels in the Red Sea and by recruiting Yemenis to fight for Russia in Ukraine.[7] Russia has also provided targeting intelligence to support Iranian strikes on Saudi Arabia and other regional targets during this current conflict, according to unspecified sources speaking to the Associated Press.[8] Russian intelligence sharing with Iran and its partners in this war demonstrates how Russia is directly supporting Iranian and Iranian-backed efforts to disrupt global energy markets and raise the cost of oil.[9]

    Iranian Domestic Affairs

    The Mobarizoun Popular Front (MPF), which is a coalition of Baloch anti-Iranian regime groups, recently established a temporary mobile checkpoint to more aggressively target regime security personnel and undermine regime authority in Sistan and Baluchistan Province. MPF published footage that showed about five fighters stopping cars and taking their information at an unspecified location on the Khash-Saravan road on October 25.[10] Several of the fighters were armed, including one with a rocket-propelled grenade. The MPF claimed that its fighters “controlled” the main transit route for “a few hours with the aim of trying to arrest” regime security personnel.[11] The MPF established a similar temporary checkpoint on the Sarbaz–Rask road in Sistan and Baluchistan Province in January 2026.[12] The MPF’s ability to establish such “checkpoints” openly in broad daylight suggests that the group assessed that security forces would be unlikely to disrupt such activities in this area, either due to a lack of capability or unwillingness.

    The MPF published the footage of the operation on their Telegram account, likely to generate fear among regime officials and affiliates and create the conditions to deter civilians from working with the Iranian regime in Sistan and Baluchistan Province.[13] MPF operations targeting regime security personnel can generate fear among the populace that working with the regime, or even cooperating with it, will make such civilians targets. Regime security forces presumably rely on local informants for information on MPF fighters’ activities and locations, and MPF checkpoints could be part of a larger effort intended to, over time, discourage residents in these areas in southeastern Iran from cooperating with the government in order to hinder regime counterinsurgency efforts and set conditions for the group to expand its base and operations. The MPF may also simultaneously be attempting to increase the group’s public visibility in order to recruit more fighters.

    Iranian security forces continue to conduct counterterrorism operations in Sistan and Baluchistan Province to arrest cells of likely MPF or MPF-affiliated anti-regime fighters. Iranian forces most recently arrested an unspecified number of fighters in Saravan and Iranshahr on October 27.[14] It remains unclear if any of the fighters arrested in Saravan were involved in establishing the Khash-Saravan checkpoint on October 25. ISW-CTP has recorded 15 MPF attacks against regime security personnel in October 2026 (see graph below).

    Arabian Peninsula

    The Houthis struck Aden International Airport on October 28, likely as part of an ongoing effort to degrade the government’s ability to sustain military operations against the Houthis.[15] The Houthis struck Aden International Airport on October 28 with at least two ballistic missiles after issuing a warning on social media to evacuate the airport.[16] The Houthis have repeatedly attacked Aden International Airport in recent weeks in order to disrupt Saudi weapons shipments to a Yemeni government military base that is co-located at the airport.[17] The Houthis may also be targeting the airport in an effort to deter future weapons shipments to Aden and prevent Saudi partners from supporting operations against the Houthis. Pakistan reportedly delivered military aid to Yemeni government-aligned forces at Aden International Airport in September, for example.[18] The Houthis may calculate that a higher risk to Saudi and partner forces’ personnel and equipment at Aden International Airport would deter these forces from resupplying Yemeni government-aligned forces in Aden.

    Yemeni government-aligned forces continued to engage Houthi fighters positioned south and southwest of Taiz City on October 28. Geolocated footage published by the Houthis shows the Houthis firing an anti-tank guided missile at a Yemeni government tank on the outskirts of Taiz City from mountainous terrain southwest of the city.[19] The footage also showed Houthi fighters operating at the Hangar Checkpoint, which lies on a main roadway leading into Taiz City from the southwest.[20] Yemeni government-aligned forces separately announced that they repelled Houthi attacks south of Taiz City in al Misrakh and Sabr al Mawadim districts on October 28.[21]
